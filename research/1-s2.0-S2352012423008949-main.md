A B S T R A C T
The performance and quality of 3D printing, as well as the mechanical properties of the final part, depend on its
printing parameters, such as layer thickness, printing speed, infill density, filling pattern, printing material, and
many others. In this study, the influence of printing parameters on the mechanical properties of the final printed
part is investigated, and at the end, a multi-objective optimization was performed on the experimental responses.
To study the effects of printing parameters, the Design of Experiments methodology was used, where five pa­
rameters considered essential were evaluated: layer thickness, printing speed, infill density, filling pattern, and
printing material. Once everything was printed, mechanical tests of traction, compression, and bending were
used to obtain the experimental curves. After obtaining the experimental responses by extracting the test curves,
the ANOVA method was applied, through which it was possible to obtain a behavior equation, a relation of the
main effects, and Pareto graphs of each experimental result on the studied parameters. Finally, the multi-
objective optimization allowed obtaining printing parameters that allowed improvements in several mechani­
cal properties in addition to the minimization of time and total mass. Results indicated a reduction of 72.39% in
the average time of printing and an increase of 9.06% in the mass after the optimization where the mechanical
properties were substantially optimized.

The growth of additive manufacturing use in several sectors is a fact.
Different researches reveal its implementation in areas such as health
and prostheses; structural and numerical optimization; and industrial
and construction [32].
In the studies by Kamila Batkuldinova et al. [4], a review on the
development of a 3D printer printed exoskeleton that addresses different
aspects such as the existing exoskeleton types, as well as their biomed­
ical applications, and then the future perspectives of 3D printing in the
development of an exoskeleton. The study reveals that the application of
additive manufacturing technology will reduce the cost of the rehabili­
tation process, which will make it more personalized and comfortable,
and will help to ease the difficulties of everyday life. In addition to that,
there is an information gap on the mechanical properties of different
materials, selection of 3D printers suitable for the job, as well as software
development in the following years. Ran Xiao et al. [33]studied 3D

human jaw, and the distribution of the porosity gradient of the printed
part in relation to the stress distribution of the jaw was calculated. The
work showed the possibility of implementing 3D printing in this field,
but further studies are still needed in order to validate this possibility.
Additive manufacturing, or 3D printing, has emerged as a versatile
technology with numerous applications in various industries. Mangla
et al. [15] emphasized the importance of Design for Additive
Manufacturing (DfAM) in optimizing 3D printing parameters to maxi­

enhancing ecological performance, resource efficiency, and the transi­
tion to a circular economy.
Equally important, Kumar & Singh [13] explores the fabrication of
surgical instruments using the FDM process. This study acknowledges
the urgent need for customized functional products that can be effi­
ciently produced through 3D printing. FDM, as a low-cost and rapid

2. Theoretical background
In this work, a study on an additive manufacturing method, 3D
printing, is detailed. This study aims to analyze and optimize the rela­
tionship of 3D printing parameters, present in the CURA® software, with
the mechanical properties of the final part. The data collected through
the mechanical tests of traction, compression and bending allowed the
analysis to be carried out using the analysis of variance (ANOVA) and
Design of Experiments (DoE) methodologies, made using Minitab®.
Finally, an optimization of the results was made, in order to simplify and
facilitate future works and studies.

Molten Deposition Modeling: here, polymer filaments are used for the
fabrication and production of the objects; ii) Selective Laser Sintering:
production is made from granulated materials of ceramics, plastics and

Several advantages can be observed regarding the use of additive
manufacturing, such as: cost, speed, customization, sustainability and
capacity of manufacturing of complex structures. As seen earlier, there
are different types of additive manufacturing. However, all of them have
stages or steps to be followed to produce the physical model: i) Digital
Modeling: In order to produce the physical model, you first need a
digital model made in some 3D design software. This step defines both
the part design and the actual measurements of the physical model; ii)
Definition of printing parameters: once the digital model is finalized, it is
necessary to transfer the project to a software called “slicer”, a name
given because 3D printings are made layer by layer. It defines the
printing parameters and then finalizes the file for printing; and iii) 3D
printing: ultimately, with the final file already in the printer system,
there is the 3D printing of the physical model. The printing time varies
according to the complexity, part size and quality desired, and can vary
from a few hours to even days of printing. In this work, as 3D printing
was used for the test specimens, the additive manufacturing present is
the Fused Deposition Modeling.
3D printers are basically composed of an extrusion nozzle that can
move in three directions (x, y and z), and also allows the use of one or
more types of feeding materials (printing filament). The design of the
desired part must be drawn in 3D modeling software such as AutoCAD®
or SolidWorks®. This modeling file can be converted to Stereo­
lithography (STL) format, which the printer can read. The part is pro­
duced layer by layer, where the extrusion nozzle is guided by the 3D
CAD model and the STL file.
The quality of the printed part depends on the parameters involved
in printing, such as infill density; printing speed; layer thickness;
extrusion nozzle temperature; etc. There are several studies on the
optimization of the production-parameter ratio for the printing of high
quality parts. Nu˜nez et al. [18] studied the dimensional accuracy and
surface texture of ABS parts. The results show that low layer thicknesses
and high filling densities are favorable for better surface quality. On the
other hand, high layer thicknesses and filling densities influence better
dimensional accuracy. Baich et al. [3] analyzed the effects of filling
pattern and printing time on the mechanical properties of ABS parts. As
expected, it was verified that when saving time and cost with the infill
density, the mechanical properties decreased. In addition, certain pat­
terns were directly related to cost and time savings, due to the amount
deposited in printing. The work by Dudescu & Racz (2017) [8] finds a
significant influence of layer thickness, extrusion nozzle temperature
and filling pattern on mechanical properties, such as tensile strength,
Young’s modulus and stiffness.
Equally important, Chamil Abeykoon et al. [1] evaluated the effects
of infill density, filling pattern, printing speed, extrusion nozzle tem­
perature, and material. It was observed that parts with 100% density
have the highest values of Young’s modulus, but the properties decrease
as the density decreases. Furthermore, for the PLA material, an ideal
temperature of 215 ◦C was found. The printing speed with the best result
was 90 mm/s, having the lowest porosity among the tests. In the works
of Ramesh Raju et al., (2020) [26] the performance of the PLA material is
evaluated in relation to three parameters: infill density, layer thickness
and printing speed. For optimized results of print time, thickness and
part stiffness, values of 60% for infill density, 0.15 mm for layer thick­
ness, and 75 mm/s were used. Pooja et al. [25] sought to optimize the
printing parameters for PLA material, then evaluated the density and
filling pattern, printing speed and layer thickness. The results, such as
printing time, material consumption and surface stiffness, show that the
density and filling pattern and layer thickness are relevant parameters in
the performance of 3D printing.
On the other hand, printing speed has minimal influence on the final
result. When studying the influences of layer thickness, extrusion nozzle
temperature, printing speed and table temperature on the mechanical
properties and dimensional accuracy of PLA parts, Yang Lyu et al. [14]
concluded that for dimensional accuracy, all results were satisfactory.
And for the mechanical properties there were values such as: 0.15 mm
for layer thickness; 50 mm/s for printing speed; 200 ◦C for the extrusion
nozzle temperature and 50 ◦C for the table temperature. The studies

2.2. Experimental planning
DoE is a statistical approach with the objective of optimizing the
process or product studied through the variation of different factors or
parameters, thus allowing the selection of factors and optimal values for
that process or product. DoE is applied, which is summarized as follows:
the selected parameters (considered important) are varied from their
lowest to the highest value and all possible combinations between the
set of parameters.
For example, a three-parameter DoE with two levels can be repre­
sented by Fig. 1.
It can be seen that the Fig. 1 shows the combination of the three

Several types of DoE approaches are possible, each of which uses a
different method of analyzing the main parameters. Some types of DoE
are: full factorial, Taguchi, Central Composite Design and Box-Behnken.
In our case, the Fractional Factorial was chosen due to the number of
tests required for the analysis.
This work uses the Response Surface Methodology (RSM), which
explores the relationships between several explanatory variables and
one or more response variables. This method uses a sequence of exper­
iments to obtain an optimized response. The idea here is to use a poly­
nomial model to describe a surface and then find the optimal answer
through approximation.

2.3. Multi-objective optimization
Optimization is a process that aims to use methods, mostly compu­
tational, to provide the user with the best answer or path for a given
circumstance. This answer can be composed of a single factor, or a
combination of factors and values of the problem variables, where the
objectives and constraints of the project are always considered. In gen­
eral, it can be said that optimization is the fact of minimizing (or
maximizing)
an
objective
function
that
presents
restrictions
[2,12,21,22,28].
According to Ticona [31], it is said that the vast majority of real
problems in the area of optimization are involved in obtaining different
goals that must be achieved at the same time, which, therefore, are
usually conflicting, thus generating the non-existence of a single solu­
tion that optimizes all the different goals. Problems involving this
description are called multi-objective optimization problems, in which
cases the analyst or project owner is responsible for analyzing and
pondering the goals and objectives, and then choosing one of the
optimal solutions from the set provided by the optimization.
Optimization is justified because this method always seeks and
achieves positive impacts on results, such as economic, performance,
competitive, quality, among others [7].
Following Pantuza Júnior [19], multi-objective optimization
methods can be classified into three categories in general:
• A-priori method or decision making before the search: here the
project owner participates in the search for solutions before solving
the problem, being able to assign elements of preference to the
objectives;
• A-posteriori method or decision-making after the search: in this
case the decision is made after the search for solutions has been
carried out, thus being considered the same relevance for all the
objectives of the problem;
• Interactive method or decision making during the search: an
interference during the solution search process is made by the project
analyst, thus directing the search in a region that is more likely to
have better solutions.
Finally, in this study, the a-priori multi-objective optimization
method was used, where the main elements and goals were defined
before carrying out the search for the optimized result.
3. DoE-based approach to 3D printing
Additive Manufacturing (AM), or 3D printing, has emerged as a
revolutionary manufacturing technology with vast potential. However,
achieving optimal performance and quality in 3D printed parts requires
a thorough understanding of the printing parameters and their impact
on the final product. In this study, a Design of Experiments (DOE)-based
approach to investigate the influence of printing parameters on the
mechanical properties of 3D printed parts is proposed. The aim is to
optimize these parameters to enhance performance while minimizing
time, material usage, and environmental impact. For this, the biblio­
graphic research aimed to guide on which are the most studied and
relevant printing parameters in the quality of the final part. Thus, it was
possible to define an acceptable amount of initial parameters to study,
since there are numerous parameters for the definition of 3D printing.
The software used was Ultimaker CURA® 4.11.0, which is one of several
software available for defining the parameters.

3.1. Design of experiments (DoE)
As shown earlier, different types of DoE are used for the analyses.
One of the most used is the Full Factorial DoE, which allows measuring
responses in all combinations of factor levels. For example, for a com­
plete 2-level factorial it is 2 k, where k is the number of factors. Therefore
k = 5, so there would be 25 = 32 experiments for a complete factorial.
Since three mechanical tests were aimed, there are three different
types of test specimens. That is, you must print 96 test specimens in
total. Since each print takes an average of 2 h to be completely printed,
the printing time would occupy a large part of the study, which would
make it impossible. In addition, inconveniences in printing, unforeseen
events, and limited laboratory use should be considered. All these fac­
tors influenced the decision to use the Fractional Factorial Design (FFD),
which is characterized by the use of only a selected subset or a “fraction”
of the tests in the complete factorial experiment. In general, FFD re­
quires only half of the experiments, so 16 experiments were targeted,
then being more viable in terms of time.
In order to obtain intermediate information, not only about the
maximum and minimum of each factor, 4 experiments were added with
intermediate values for each factor studied.
For the analysis of this work, five factors (decision variables) were
considered, along with their minimum and maximum values, as shown
in Table 2. Such values were obtained from studies mentioned above,
which have similar proposals to this study. Table 3 exemplifies and
details the experiments and values.

4.4. Multi-objective optimization results
Once the regression equations (with high R2) are obtained from the
design of experiments, optimization studies can be used. At this point,
the previous equations were used, evaluating eight different optimiza­
tion cases considering 3 objectives. In all optimization cases, the mass
and time responses were kept constant in two of the objectives, and each
of the eight responses of properties obtained in flexion, compression and
tension were varied in each case.
To solve the cases created, the Multiobjective Lichtenberg Algorithm
(MOLA) [24] was used. The control parameters used in the algorithm
were population of 200 individuals, refinement ref = 0.40, number of
particles in the Lichtenberg figure Np = 104
, Stick probability Sc = 1,
creation radius Rc = 150 e total iterations Niter = 100. The iteration limit
was set as the stopping criterion in all cases.
Fig. 12 shows the final result of the multiobjective optimization in
each of the eight cases evaluated. The non-dominated solutions that
form the Pareto surface obtained are highlighted. The TOPSIS technique
(Technique for Order of Preference by Similarity to Ideal Solution) [11]
was used as a decision-making technique in order to choose the optimal
solution among the hundreds available solutions on the Pareto surface.
As a weighting, equal weights were defined for each of the three ob­
jectives evaluated (that is, w = 0.33). Table 9 shows the TOPSIS solution
for each case evaluated and the responses obtained for this respective
solution.
In general, in all cases of optimization the responses were optimized
when compared to the initial design of the experiments (factorial
points). It is noteworthy that in all cases the mass and time were
significantly optimized. In terms of time, a 95.02% reduction in the
overall average printing time was achieved while the mechanical
properties were improved (optimized). Furthermore, a reduction in
mass (average) of 8.05% was obtained in relation to the factorial
experimental design. It is also noteworthy that the reduction in mass and
time could be greater if only the mono-objective optimization was
considered. These (already significant) reductions were obtained
simultaneously with the optimization of mechanical properties, since
there are no gains without losses in the multiobjective context.
The filling pattern obtained was in most cases Hexagonal and the
material did not have a clear definition (alternating between ABS and
PLA depending on each case to be optimized). Regarding the continuous
variables of the process (speed, thickness and density), the results ob­
tained were within the axial limits of the factorial arrangement. In some
cases, the optimal point fell at the bounded point, especially regarding
the layer thickness and infill density variables. The predominant pattern
found was the hexagonal type and the gyroid pattern was significant in
only two of the optimal cases. It is noteworthy that these cases were
obtained in only one of the 1000 possible points of non-dominated so­
lutions. Possibly, when considering different weights (importance) in
the objectives, other optimal parameters of the process would come into
consideration. Regarding the material, the ABS and PLA filaments
alternated in the optimal solutions. Depending on the application, one
material will perform better than another.
5. Conclusions
This study employed a Design of Experiments approach to analyze
the statistical significance of various printing parameters in additive
manufacturing. Through extensive experimentation, we established
linear regression equations with high accuracy (R2 > 95%) to describe
the physical behavior of the experimental responses. Subsequently,
multiobjective optimization was performed, aiming to minimize time
and mass while maximizing mechanical properties.
The analyses revealed a strong correlation among most of the
experimental responses, posing challenges for the multi-objective opti­
mization phase. However, it was identified that the time and mass of the
structure exhibited independent correlations, enabling their optimiza­
tion alongside one additional response of interest among the eight me­
chanical properties tested in tension, compression, and bending.
Notably, significant improvements were achieved in both time and mass
optimization, with a remarkable reduction of 95.02% in average print­
ing time.
While the filling pattern tended to be predominantly hexagonal, and
the choice of material (ABS or PLA) influenced performance in traction,
compression, and bending, the continuous variables (speed, thickness,
and density) were optimized within the experimental limits. Further­
more, it is important to highlight the trade-off between improved me­
chanical response, which requires more time and material consumption,
and reduced response when minimizing these factors.
To gain a deeper understanding of the influence of printing param­
eters on mechanical properties and to optimize values for specific ap­
plications, future research should encompass a broader range of
parameters, considering the numerous possibilities in additive
manufacturing. Additionally, exploring other parameters of the additive
manufacturing process and studying more complex structures under
diverse loading conditions would be valuable directions for further
investigation.
In conclusion, this study successfully employed DoE and multi-
