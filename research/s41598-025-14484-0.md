Surface appearance of poly lactic
acid due to variations in material
extrusion processing parameters
Roberto Spina
1,2,3
The study examines the impact of Material Extrusion (MEX) process parameters on the surface
quality of polylactic acid (PLA) components, utilizing a straightforward image analysis method for
distinguishing products realized with varying process parameter sets. The research aims to establish
a relationship between process variations and product characteristics, with a primary focus on surface
roughness and grayscale variations. Process parameters such as flow rate, nozzle temperature,
and fan speed significantly influence the surface roughness and appearance of PLA parts. These
parameters alter the shape of raster deposition, resulting in a different peak-valley arrangement,
which in turn impacts surface quality and grayscale variations. The study evaluates grayscale image
analysis to assess color shifts induced by processing conditions, for a scanning direction perpendicular
to the printing direction. The results of grayscale analysis were compared with surface roughness,
achieving a significant match. Variations in surface roughness and grayscale values were substantial,
ranging between 50% and 60%. The PLA specimens were finally tested under uniaxial tensile tests to
determine Young’s modulus, tensile strength, and strain at break, evaluating changes in mechanical
performance. The flow rate and fan speed were crucial factors because they caused changes in raster
density, raster adhesion, and mechanical properties. However, the mechanical test results revealed
that the variations were more significant than 5% but lower than 10%.
Keywords  Material extrusion, Material testing, Color, Polylactic acid.
Material extrusion (MEX) is an Additive Manufacturing (AM) process in which a thermoplastic polymer is
dispensed through a heated nozzle in a continuous stream and deposited layer by layer to build a 3D object1.
The nozzle moves along a plane, and a platform moves vertically after realizing each new layer. The first layer
is the first slice of the cross-sectional area of the object, while the following layers are added on top of the
previous ones. Layers are fused upon deposition as the material is in a melted state. MEX is most commonly used
in 3D printing processes due to its advantages, including ease of manufacturing, readily available technology,
and lower production costs. Still, its mature technology and the possibility of using several widespread and
inexpensive raw thermoplastics make MEX one of the most popular AM processes2. The main limitations are
related to shortcomings, such as defects and anisotropy, low surface finish, and dimensional variations of printed
parts induced by the fixed nozzle and feed diameter. Depending on their dimension, the layer-by-layer patterns
made with a settled thickness partially mask fine features3.
In manufacturing and industrial environments, non-functional prototypes or cost-effective rapid parts and
toolings are realized with MEX. Several authors have investigated the mechanical properties of MEX parts
by varying technological parameters, despite the absence of appropriate standards specifically designed to
compare these properties with those of conventional products4. Many parameters evolved, and their variation
could significantly alter the mechanical characteristics of MEX components. These process parameters were
classified into slicing factors, building orientation, and temperature conditions5. Process parameters controlled
the attributes of MEX products, such as strength and deformation. The impact of these parameters on the
characteristics of MEX parts and components is still gaining popularity6. Several approaches have been employed
in the literature to correlate process parameters with the performance of polylactic acid (PLA) parts. Statistical
analysis was used to connect material characteristics, mechanical properties, and printing parameters. The results
evidenced that optimized parameters, such as print speed, layer thickness, and temperature, directly impacted
the final properties, with an overall dimensional accuracy of 98% and superior mechanical properties to injection
molding products7. The effects of layer thickness and orientation on the mechanical properties, especially the

main and interaction effects of layer thickness and printing orientation on the mechanical properties using two
infill density rates. In the case of fully dense specimens, increasing layer thickness and orienting filaments in
the direction of the applied load greatly improved mechanical strength. However, the printed material became
more brittle8. The MEX process parameters, such as the feed rate and nozzle temperature, significantly affected
the interlayer bonding and tensile strength, establishing a direct link between increased feed rates and reduced
interlayer adhesion, as well as the positive impact of higher nozzle temperatures on material cohesion9.
Another crucial aspect to consider is surface quality. MEX surfaces comprised various spatial frequency
factors: profile, form, waviness, and roughness. Each factor had a proper origin and explicitly influenced
the product’s appearance and performance. The profile strictly depended on the layer-by-layer processes;
the waviness revealed machine vibration; the poor performance of the machine usually produced the form;
surface irregularities generated the roughness due to printing and material removal errors10. Surface roughness
was a critical texture component for assessing the quality of manufactured items, as it involved investigating
the distribution of topographical features on the surface. Surface roughness was one of the essential factors
in evaluating the quality of mechanical products, especially their functionality and assembly at mechanical
interfaces, ensuring the proper functioning of precision parts. Lower post-treatment costs for higher surface
qualities were highly desired. Product surface roughness changed drastically during printing when the extruder
was under- or over-extruded11. Surface roughness within specifications was critical to improve the MEX process.
Different post-processing techniques and parameter adjustments were available to control surface roughness,
which resulted in increased processing costs. If the printing parameters or material could be debugged and the
desired results were achieved, printing time and economic costs would be significantly reduced12.
The variation of the MEX process parameters was also used to generate colors in a single mono-color
filament. An example was the manufacture of structural colors thanks to their low cost, reliability, and ease of
use. The combination of 3D printing technology and photonic crystal structural color materials was essential.
Rainbow colors were generated by periodic sub-micron or nano-structured photonic crystals. Amorphous
photonic crystals, formed by the periodic arrangement of the particle structure, generated non-rainbow colors13.
Operating on the MEX process, 3D-printed pyrography realized multiple shades of color and gradients using a
wood-based PLA filament extruded from a single-nozzle printer. 3D printed artifacts with various and distinct
shades of a single color were realized, altering the color appearance. The dedicated MEX software controlled
the nozzle temperature during the process, dynamically generating the G-code14. An alternative method for
creating color variations utilizes a MEX multi-nozzle machine. One nozzle was responsible for 3D printing,
whereas a second nozzle reheated the printed areas at varying speeds, controlling the material’s temperature
response. Rapid speed adjustments enabled fine-grained reheating, allowing for high-resolution color and
texture variations15. The variation of process parameters in fabricating transparent parts was also possible. Some
process parameters, such as layer height, road width, and deposition speed, were varied, and their effects on the
transmittance and spatial resolution were examined16.
Currently, MEX printers are open-loop control systems, which means they are unable to monitor the
information during the printing process, process the data promptly, and take corresponding compensation
measures. This study investigates greyscale image analysis to evaluate the influence of MEX process parameters on
surface color. It proposes a simple method for differentiating products obtained with different process parameter
sets and a preliminary framework for closed-loop surface analysis. The experimental protocol for acquiring
sample images, allowing image reconstruction, is initially described. Then, the image processing developed
using gray image analysis is detailed, and the results are analyzed using various factors (value, distribution,
shape) to validate the method. Lastly, mechanical tests on the MEX specimens with different process parameter
sets are performed to identify the main tensile properties.
Materials and methods
PLA is a biodegradable and renewable aliphatic polyester, replacing conventional petrochemical-based
polymers14. It is easy to sand, drill, or cut after printing, and can be easily pigmented and dyed with acrylic-
based paints, oil, or cellulose spray paints17. Forward AM Technologies GmbH (Heidelberg, Germany) supplied
a 1.75 mm PLA filament with a diameter of 1.75 mm; primary data are reported in Table 1.
The specimens were modelled using Autodesk Fusion 2603.1.15 (San Francisco, USA), sliced with OrcaSlicer
2.3.0 (SoftFever), and then realized using the Bambu Lab X1 Carbon, a MEX printer produced by Shenzhen
Tuozhu Technology Co. (Shenzhen, China). The MEX architecture of this printer was based on the CoreXY design,
where two motors simultaneously controlled the X and Y axes. The build volume was 256 × 256 × 256 mm³. All
dog bone samples were designed using ISO 527-2:201219, with envelope dimensions of 150 × 20 × 4 mm3 (Fig. 1).
They were printed with a layer height of 200 μm, an infill of 100%, and an internal alternative 0°/90° linear
pattern, with three layers allocated for the top, bottom, and walls (Fig. 2). The 100% solid fill density was selected
as the most desirable material density for functional components requiring optimal mechanical properties. The

Due to the multiple settings of MEX process parameters, specimen type 1B was needed for a long path to
achieve different cooling conditions. Three specimens with varying process parameters were printed flat on the
build platform (XY plane), one specimen at a time, to reduce the influence of environmental conditions.
Design and process parameters varied according to a Box-Behnken Design (BBD) plan, a class of rotatable
or nearly rotatable second-order designs based on three-level incomplete factorial designs20. The number of
experiments N required by a BBD plan was equal to 2×k×(k − 1) + C0, with k the number of factors and C0 the
number of central points. The number of experiments for a Central Composite Design was 2k + 2k + C0, greater
than BBD. The BBD did not contain combinations in which all factors were simultaneously at their highest
or lowest levels. Thus, this design helped avoid experiments performed under extreme conditions that might
produce unsatisfactory results. Conversely, it was not indicated for situations in which the responses at the
extremes should be known. Table 2 reports process parameters related to specimen fabrication, identified through
preliminary tests aimed at achieving stable flow conditions. The selected ranges of the process parameters Q, Tn,
and F were the most common for PLA processing.

attached to it. The attached fan increased heat dissipation by directing the fan speed F toward the filament feed
direction, thereby preventing excess heat from reaching the pinch-feed mechanism above the print head21. The

The surface characteristics were acquired using a Conoscan 3000 (Oproprint srl, Rome, Italy), a laser-based
conoscopic holographic (LBCH) sensor with a distortion-free aspheric lens of 25 mm focal length and an optical
accuracy of less than 1 μm. The visible light source was a 655 nm laser diode. The laser sensor acquisition was
performed on a rectangular surface patch measuring 10 × 10 mm². The working frequency, representing the data
acquisition rate, was 2,500 Hz, and the power level, representing the value of the laser beam energy, was 4,000.
The surface texture was measured offline using MountainMap (Digital Surf, Besancon, France) according to
the definitions of ISO 25178-2:202225. The ROI area was 5 × 5 mm2. As an optical technique, the LBCH process
could be affected by the surface slope and its position within the working range. To avoid these influencing
factors, the specimens were firmly positioned on the machine table using a customized fixture, ensuring the
test surfaces remained parallel to the XY plane of the scanner at a distance equal to the sensor standoff (Fig. 3).
The main directions of the test specimens were also aligned with the machine XY axes. The selected region was
digitized, and measurements were performed continuously, starting from a corner, moving transversely to the
specimen length, and repositioning to perform the next pass. The scanning direction was perpendicular to the
printing direction. The density of points captured was 10 μm in each direction.
The specimen photos were then acquired using a digital single-lens reflex (DSLR) camera, the EOS 760D,
with a 24.2-million-pixel APS-C CMOS sensor and an EF-S 60 mm f/2.8 Macro USM lens (Canon Inc., Tokyo,
Japan). The EOS utility communicated with the digital camera to display and download images, shoot remotely,
and control each setting (Fig. 4). The camera settings and light conditions were the same for all the photos.
The images were elaborated using Fiji software, an open-source platform for biological-image analysis26, and
MountainMap. The acquired photos, measuring 6,000 pixels × 4,000 pixels, were captured with a directional
light source, filtered, and de-noised to obtain the images with improved accuracy to convert into 8-bit images
(grayscale). Grayscale was the most straightforward model, defining colors using only intensity, ranging from 0
(black) to 255 (white). The photos were then cropped to mark the Region of Interest (ROI) of 650 pixels × 950
pixels at the center of the specimen. The resulting conversion scale between pixels and mm was set to 76 pixels,
equal to 1 mm, obtaining an ROI area of 5 × 5 mm2. The gray value of each pixel within the ROI was measured.
The XYG data were exported as an ordered text file for subsequent statistical analyses, where X and Y represented
the point coordinates, and G represented the gray value. The density of points captured was 13 μm in each
direction.
The mechanical properties of the MEX samples were evaluated using a uniaxial tensile test, which allowed
for the characterization of their elastic and plastic behavior under monotonic loading. Mechanical tests on dog-
bone specimens were conducted on an ETM type A universal single-column testing machine (Wance Testing
Machine Co., Ltd., Shenzhen, China) with a 5 kN load cell (accuracy of ± 0.5%) and a crosshead travel (without
grip) of 1,000 mm (accuracy of ± 0.5%). A strain gage-type extensometer (Wance EX02510) with a gauge length
of 25 mm and a maximum travel of 10 mm was used to measure the longitudinal strain by employing knife edges
that came into contact with the sample (Fig. 5). According to ISO 527-1:201927, measurements were taken in a
temperature-controlled environment with a transverse speed of 5 mm/min and a maximum variation of ± 0.5 °C
from the ambient temperature. Five repetitions were performed per set, and the results were averaged.

Results and discussion
The total number of PLA specimens generated was 17, with k = 3 and Co = 5. The developed DoE plan is shown
in Table 3.
All MEX specimens were in good condition; no layer delamination, warping, or material degradation was
detected. However, as Fig. 6 shows, the color difference and surface aspects between specimens of different sets
were evident through a simple visual inspection. The variations in process parameters caused the color difference.
It was perceptibly apparent that the white-black shift and intensity increased with the permanence time of the
material in the melt condition and the raster dimensions. As expected, a low deposition time was associated with
thicker material accumulation, resulting in a well-defined deposition raster with a smaller width. The peaks of
each raster had higher gray values than the surrounding valleys. On the contrary, the rasters realized with high
deposition were thinner, with a larger width, and exhibited minor variations between peaks and valleys.
Root mean squared height Sq and arithmetical mean height Sa were the metrics used in MountainMap to
measure the surface texture, as they were not sensitive to measurement parameters such as sampling length and
evaluation length28. Sq represented the root mean square of ordinate values within the defined ROI of area A,
equivalent to the root mean square deviation Rq. Sa was the extension to the surface of a line’s arithmetical mean
height Ra, expressing, as an absolute value, the difference in height of each point compared to the arithmetical
mean of the surface. These parameters were generally used to evaluate surface roughness. The expressions Sq-Z
and Sa-Z are reported below, where the subscript Z is associated with the LBCH acquisition.

Conclusions
This study highlighted a strict relationship between online analysis of surface grayscale and offline measurements
of surface roughness. The correct selection of process parameters in MEX 3D printing improved the surface
roughness and color of PLA components. The results provided practical guidance for adjusting printing
parameters, such as nozzle temperature, flow rate, and fan speed, to achieve products with the desired grayscale
variation while minimizing the impact on mechanical properties. The study highlighted that printing parameters
significantly influenced the surface appearance of MEX components, with variations of 50–60% in color and
different value distributions. The results indicated higher flow rates and nozzle temperatures decreased surface
roughness metrics. Optimized deposition patterns enhanced mechanical strength by increasing raster density
and bonding. Color variations were found to be influenced by cooling conditions and raster configurations,
suggesting a potential grayscale-based quality assessment. Moreover, the mechanical test results revealed that
the significant variations in surface roughness and color affected mechanical performance by less than 10%.
The results suggested implementing future closed-loop control systems to monitor and correct real-time
deviations during the printing process. The main idea will be to act directly on the G-code to define part color
(and indirect roughness) and then adjust the process parameters. This approach will allow the achievement of
Zero Defect Manufacturing, resulting in defect-free products. Process monitoring will enable the assessment
of whether a product satisfies specific requirements. In-situ inspection techniques will fundamentally increase
customer confidence in a product and reduce costs due to rejection, as process anomalies will be detected
immediately after they occur.
