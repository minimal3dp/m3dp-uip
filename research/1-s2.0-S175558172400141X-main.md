A B S T R A C T
In this study, an analysis was conducted to quantify errors in the additive manufacturing process, with a focus on
comparing data files. The primary objective was to minimise the reliance on physical testing of produced
components by favouring verification within a virtual environment. The initial focus was on the conversion from
the CAD (Computer-Aided Design) model to the STL (Standard Tessellation Language) file, where discrepancies
between the two formats were identified. To achieve optimal meshing, it is crucial to configure conversion
parameters effectively, avoiding both detail loss and the handling of excessively large files. Following this, a
comparison between STL files and reconstructed G-code files was conducted, uncovering further approximations
introduced by the most commonly used slicers. In conclusion, the analysis highlights that both the quality of the
mesh and the slicing phase significantly impact the final component. Understanding these factors is essential for
achieving an optimal print outcome.
1. Introduction
1.1. FFF 3D printing
Additive manufacturing (AM) represents an advanced technology
that relies on a series of digital conversion processes essential for the
creation of three-dimensional objects from CAD models [1]. Various AM
processes exist, but this discussion will focus on the material extrusion
(MEX) process, which is the most widely used method globally. These
conversion stages, though indispensable, inevitably introduce errors
that compromise dimensional accuracy and the overall quality of the
final product. In particular, the two critical conversion phases [2]—the
transformation of the CAD model into an STL file [3], followed by the
conversion of the STL file into G-code—are subject to intrinsic inac-
curacies that require careful consideration [4,5].
The CAD model, which represents the geometry of the product
through mathematically defined curved surfaces, is approximated in an
STL file using a mesh of planar triangles. This discretisation process
generates an approximation error known as the chordal error, defined as
the distance between the original CAD surface and the approximated
STL surface [6,7]. The density of the STL facets directly affects this error:
a higher density improves the approximation of the original surface but
also increases the file size and computational load. Therefore, selecting
the appropriate conversion parameters during this stage is crucial to
minimise information loss and preserve the model’s quality.
The subsequent phase, involving the conversion of the STL file into
G-code, is equally critical [8]. In this step, the model is sliced into thin
layers, and the necessary tool paths for material deposition are gener-
ated layer by layer [9]. The slicing resolution, determined by the layer
thickness, plays a fundamental role in the accuracy of the physical
representation of the object. Additionally, the algorithms used to
generate the tool paths [10], which dictate the movement of the
extruder [11], directly influence the dimensional accuracy, surface fin-
ish, and mechanical properties of the final component.
The quality of the STL file is a key factor in this phase: if the STL file is
imprecise, the lost information cannot be recovered, thus compromising
the final product’s quality. Furthermore, the printing parameters
selected in the slicing software[12–14], which control various aspects
such as temperature, cooling, material deposition[15], and the selection
of the optimal tool path, further impact the final outcome. The scientific
literature has extensively documented the influence of these parameters
on print quality and the errors that can arise during the process[16,17].
In critical applications, such as in mechanical engineering or the
biomedical sector [18], where printed components must adhere to strict
tolerances, evaluating the accuracy of the tool path becomes essential.
High precision in the tool path allows for the prediction, simulation, and
management of defects and voids [19,20] within the component.

Some methods for improving the accuracy of tool path generation
[23,24] involve increasing the precision of the points used [25–27].
However, this must be supported by both the computer generating the

is excessively increased. A recently proposed solution involves con-

this necessitates a machine capable of reading it.
To optimise curved surfaces, G2 and G3 commands can be used,
which allow for the creation of clockwise and counterclockwise circular
arcs, respectively, to enhance the precision of the path and reduce the
size of the G-code [28,29]. However, these commands must be partic-
ularly well-optimised to correctly interpret the component’s geometries
and avoid errors. These instructions can be generated with certain
software directly during the slicing phase or during the post-processing
of the G-code, such as with Arc Welder [30] or Polygon Smoothing [31].
1.2. Rules for good design
Good design principles for additive manufacturing (AM) [32,33] are
crucial in optimizing the production of complex and customized parts
while navigating the inherent limitations of various AM technologies.
Effective design in AM, particularly for material extrusion, requires a
deep understanding of the specific characteristics and constraints of the
technology [34]. Key principles include the need to tailor designs to the
unique geometric capabilities and limitations of material extrusion, such
as layer-based construction, overhangs, and minimum feature sizes,
ensuring manufacturability and structural integrity [35–37].
Design guidelines must also prioritize manufacturability, robustness,
and cost-effectiveness, ensuring that parts can be produced efficiently
and perform reliably in their intended applications [38]. This includes
optimizing part orientation to minimize support structures, reducing
material usage, and improving print time, which are particularly rele-
vant in material extrusion due to its layer-by-layer deposition process
[39,40].
Moreover, effective AM design should incorporate post-processing
requirements, such as sanding and polishing in material extrusion, to
address surface finish and micro-porosity issues, thereby improving the
overall quality and mechanical properties of the part. Integrating these
considerations during the design phase is essential to ensure that the
final product meets the required specifications.
In addition, as AM increasingly intersects with remanufacturing
processes [40], design rules must evolve to address the unique chal-
lenges of additive remanufacturing. This includes the need for a tailored
approach that considers both the initial AM process and the re-
quirements for restoring or upgrading parts, thus promoting efficiency,
sustainability, and lifecycle extension in production.
2. Materials and methods
To minimize the need for physical testing of components produced
through 3D printing, a strategy is employed that involves the verifica-
tion of the component within a virtual environment. Components pro-
duced by 3D printing are inherently anisotropic due to the layered
deposition of materials and other intrinsic imperfections in the process,
as illustrated in Fig. 1. Therefore, it is essential to ensure that the final
component closely matches the designed CAD model. This verification
process takes place before the printing phase, involving the conversion
of the theoretical model into the necessary data for the manufacturing
process. Referring to Fig. 1, the process begins with the CAD model, from
which the STEP file and STL files are exported at various levels of detail
(as will be described later). The STL models are then processed in
different slicers using identical parameters to generate the correspond-
ing G-code for each STL file within each slicer. Since G-code files cannot
be directly compared with STL files, they must first be "solidified,"
meaning that a mesh will be generated as if they had been printed. The
comparisons will then be conducted between the STEP file and the STL
files, as well as between the STL files and the solidified G-code meshes.
2.1. STL conversion
Following the modelling phase, the geometry undergoes conversion
into the STL format for processing by the slicer. Resolution is typically
governed by three parameters: chord deviation, angle deviation, and
maximum face size (Fig. 2).
In the tessellation of a model, face creation occurs by utilizing points
belonging to the surface of the component. Approximation does not
affect all surfaces uniformly; flat surfaces remain unchanged regardless
of the tessellation level used. In the case of holes or internal cavities, a
reduction in diameter occurs due to tessellation using points from the
external surface of the CAD model, resulting in faces that slightly exceed
the geometry of the piece. Interpolating the surface obtained after
tessellation yields a diameter smaller than that defined in the CAD ge-
ometry (Fig. 3). Applying the same principle elucidates how the external
geometry of curved parts appears less defined compared to the CAD-
modelled geometry.
The quality of the STL file also influences the trajectory of the nozzle
generated by the slicer [41] (Fig. 4). Considering the two meshes, it is
evident that there are slight differences in deposition. While these dis-
crepancies may appear minor, they represent a non-negligible concern.
Given a unique CAD file, the qualitative and mechanical behaviour
outcomes can vary. Therefore, the conversion phase into STL files re-
quires meticulous attention to minimize cascading effects.

Slicing software is used to convert the 3D model represented in the
STL file format into specific instructions that the 3D printer can under-

2.3. Case study
The component selected for analysis is a modified motorcycle gas
cam (Fig. 5), designed to reduce wrist rotation during acceleration. This
particular geometry was chosen because it incorporates a wide array of
features commonly present in 3D-printed components, including a large
outer diameter, a small-diameter hole, a flat surface, a complex contour,
and an overhanging section. These features are vital for enabling a
thorough analysis of those aspects that may be more or less prone to
errors during the printing process. By including such a diverse set of
elements, the evaluation is able to address a broad range of potential
challenges, thereby providing a solid foundation for assessing the ac-
curacy of 3D-printed objects.

3.2. Comparison between mesh and G-code files
The G-code files were generated using the parameters listed in
Table 1 across the following slicers: Lychee Slicer 6.0.2, Orca Slicer
2.1.1, Prusa Slicer 2.8.0, and Ultimaker Cura 5.8, which are among the
most commonly used slicers for the majority of small to medium-sized
machines available on the market, in addition to being free of charge.
These slicers have the significant advantage of offering preset configu-
rations for machines from numerous manufacturers, allowing for a
quicker setup process. The G-code was specifically generated for the
Artillery Sidewinder X1, as this printer is included in the configuration
wizard of all the selected software.
The print bed within the slicer was adjusted to centre the reference
system. By placing the component on the print bed, aligned with the
centre and zeroed coordinates, slicing can be performed while main-
taining the coordinate system consistent with the STL file. This facili-
tates a more accurate comparison, as alignment errors are eliminated.
It is noteworthy that the G-code resolution was set significantly
lower than the machine’s resolution. Parameters related to the imported
STL mesh are unalterable within the software, thus remaining constant
across all G-code generation processes. To facilitate comparison within
Geomagic software, the G-code must be either a solid or a mesh.
By utilizing the Prusa G-code Viewer software, it was possible to
visualize the toolpath and the deposition-generated component (Fig. 7a,
b). This software enables the export of G-code in OBJ format, which,
while still a file containing a mesh, includes additional information
compared to the STL file.
The reconstructed G-code mesh is generated by the software,
applying a rhomboidal cross-section to the toolpath, as depicted in Fig. 7
(c). Unfortunately, this cross-sectional geometry of the deposited line is
not adjustable.
Importing the mesh into the slicer and generating the toolpath results
in a significant simplification of the profile in curved regions of the
component. This is attributed to intrinsic and unalterable parameters
within the software. Fig. 8 illustrates how the fine and dense meshes
were approximated similarly by the slicer, despite the significantly
different finishing levels in the two imported files.
To verify that the approximation was inherent in the toolpath, it was
confirmed through NC Viewer that the distance between the points was
the same as the points along the line in the mesh.
The comparison between the mesh imported into the slicer and the
mesh generated from the G-code produced the results shown in Table 4.
When examining the comparisons between "Mesh coarse – G-code
coarse" and "Mesh fine – G-code fine," it is observed that the errors are
quite similar. This suggests that the slicer introduces an error that affects
the model uniformly, regardless of its precision level. By comparing the
G-code with its original mesh, one can observe the cumulative error
introduced during this conversion process.
Each slicer analysed exhibited a reduction in the quality of the
toolpath, even when handling high-quality STL files. Despite this, the
mean error recorded is less than 0.3 millimetres, which still reflects a
commendable degree of precision in toolpath generation. From the nu-
merical data in Table 4, as well as the graphical representation in Fig. 9,
it is clear that the mean error associated with the fine mesh is the lowest
among the cases studied.
If the precision level of the STL file is exceptionally high, the slicer
reduces its precision, as illustrated in Fig. 8. Consequently, the error in
the dense mesh scenario is greater than in the other two cases. This error
includes a component shared with the other scenarios, as well as an
additional approximation component introduced by the slicer. In this
context, the slicer simplifies the dense mesh in a manner similar to the
fine mesh. As a result, the comparison in the dense mesh situation re-
veals a more significant deviation relative to the other two cases, due to
the higher precision of the reference used.
In Fig. 10 (a), the overlay comparison between the fine mesh (in
green) and the fine G-code (in blue) on an internal curved surface is
visible. The G-code mesh, having lower quality, does not perfectly
follow the profile of the surface, resulting in some points deviating from
the STL file surface. Using the STL file as a reference, positive errors
occur on internal curved surfaces, while negative errors occur on
external curved surfaces. Fig. 10 (b) illustrates this concept with a
sectional view. This phenomenon does not occur on the entire perimeter
of the component; otherwise, the average error could not be an order of
magnitude smaller. On flat lateral surfaces, the STL surface coincides
with the outer edge of the rhomboid.
To better analyse the discretisation performed by the slicer during
the generation of the G-code, certain characteristic dimensions of the
component under analysis were measured. In Fig. 11, the curved sur-
faces selected for measurement and comparison are indicated. These
surfaces were chosen due to their varying radii of curvature, allowing a
comparison between the original STL values and those of the solidified
G-code.
The points of interest were measured on the STL files with three
levels of detail, as well as on the G-codes generated by the slicers. The
average data collected is summarised in Table 5 and graphically repre-
sented in Fig. 12. All measurements were taken from the same area of
the component to ensure the analysis was not skewed. It is evident that
the fine mesh contains the greatest level of detail, as it features a smaller
face edge compared to the other two meshes. Upon examining the di-
mensions obtained from the solidified G-codes, it becomes apparent that
these values are quite similar across all cases. This finding supports the
previous analysis: even when a highly precise file is input into the slicer,
the toolpath is still simplified, leading to a loss of precision and
information.

4. Discussion
The analysis of the 3D printed components’ manufacturing process
has brought forth several noteworthy considerations. The layer-by-layer
assembly of a component introduces imperfections that can compromise
its fidelity to the reference CAD model if not conducted judiciously. The
accuracy of the STL file’s mesh has emerged as a discriminating factor
for curved surfaces. While a meticulous tessellation provides a superior
geometric representation, it generates a larger file size with longer
processing times compared to a coarse tessellation, which simplifies the
process at the expense of quality.
In the initial phase, CAD files were compared to STL files to evaluate
the nature and extent of errors introduced during the conversion
process. This analysis highlighted differences in the type and size of
errors that can arise during the discretization of the component. Given
the discretized nature of the component, it is crucial to ensure that the
approximation is not excessive for the dimensions and functionalities of
the model. The average error with a coarse mesh is on the order of
10^−3 mm, while the average error with a fine mesh is on the order of
10^−4 mm. The dense mesh exhibits an error of the same order of
magnitude as the fine mesh, but the generated file size is much larger,
posing challenges in subsequent processing steps.
The analysis of the error between the STL file and the reconstructed
G-code revealed a significant approximation of geometric details,
particularly on curved surfaces, which can be attributed to the slicer.
This issue is especially critical for complex or detailed models where
accuracy is paramount. Quantifying these theoretical inaccuracies has
highlighted the limitations of the conversion process. The mean error
between the STL and G-code remains consistent, as the toolpath gener-
ation employs the same parameters for each slice.
It is important to note that while the quality of the input file to the
slicer is significant, its impact is limited, as the slicer inevitably in-
troduces simplifications. As previously discussed, the fine mesh exhibits
a lower error level compared to the other meshes analysed. Moreover, it
is the most optimal file to manage, offering maximum quality with a
reduced file size. To create such a file, it is crucial to minimise the chord

Furthermore, the comparison between the G-code file and the STL
mesh highlighted how curved surfaces can lead to approximations and
dimensional variations. To improve the quality of curved surfaces, one
can use G2 and G3 functions, which allow for the creation of arcs,
thereby preserving the correct curvature rather than segmenting the
curves. However, the challenge with using these commands lies in
accurately interpreting what constitutes an arc and what does not. For a
curved surface to be recognised as such by the slicer, it must meet a
certain tolerance level, as the input mesh is still segmented. Adjusting
this tolerance requires modifying the resolution of the G-code. If the
precision is set too high, the software may fail to detect any curves,
leaving the code unaltered. Conversely, if the precision is set too low, the
quality of linear paths deteriorates, and the software may incorrectly
convert linear segments into arcs, significantly reducing their quality.
The graphs presented in Fig. 13 depict the error distribution starting
from the same CAD file and extending to the generation of the G-code
file, quantifying the magnitude of the error that could accumulate when
combining these effects. The pronounced difference between the pro-
cesses with fine and coarse meshes is evident, with the discriminant step
being the conversion from CAD to STL files.
5. Conclusion
The 3D printing process, while continually advancing, has yet to
attain
the
precision
and
reliability
of
traditional
subtractive
manufacturing methods. This analysis has brought to light the crucial
influence of both steps required for G-code creation on the accuracy of
the final result. In the conversion process from CAD to STL files, striking
a balance between mesh quality and overall file size is noteworthy.
Reducing chord deviation and angle deviation parameters allows for an
optimal mesh quality, with an error in the order of 10−4 millimetres. In
the transformation from mesh to G-code, a simplification of the tessel-
lation occurs due to the use of the slicer. This simplification has been
observed in slicers such as Ultimaker Cura, Prusa Slicer, Orca Slicer, and
Lychee Slicer, which are among the most commonly used, and it results
in an error of approximately 10−2 millimetres. The entire process is
significantly influenced by this second conversion, from STL to G-code,
which reduces the level of detail. Some slicers offer the option to
perform slicing directly from a STEP file, a mathematical format,
thereby eliminating the initial conversion step. However, as described,
this has a relatively minor impact on the final outcome. To enhance the
quality and precision of the G-code, using arc generation commands
(G2/G3) can be a beneficial approach, though it is essential to find a
good balance between precision and toolpath generation. In summary,
the user has the ability to intervene effectively during the conversion
from CAD file to STL to minimise errors in the subsequent stages of the
process. During the second conversion, an approximation occurs, and
there is less opportunity for intervention with commonly used slicers.
Nevertheless, when the cumulative effects are considered, the total
resulting error remains within 0.2 millimetres in the optimal scenario. It
is therefore important for the user to be aware of what happens during
the various conversion stages of their file, in order to take all necessary
precautions.
