a b s t r a c t
Fused deposition modeling (FDM) is a growing 3D printing technique widely practiced
around the world in various industrial applications because of its ability to create complex
3D objects and geometries. Reduction of build time and feedstock material consumption
without compromising the mechanical performance is the major concern in most indus-
trial applications affecting the cost and the functionality of the manufactured part. One
of the key issues of FDM process is how to select the correct parameters to reduce the
build time and to reduce feedstock material consumption while maintaining high dynamic
mechanical properties. In this study, inﬂuence of critical FDM parameters—layer thickness,
air gap, raster angle, build orientation, road width, and number of contours—are studied
using Q-optimal response surface methodology. Their effects on build time, feedstock ma-
terial consumption and dynamic ﬂexural modulus are critically examined. Mathematical
models have been formulated to develop a functional relationship between the processing
conditions and the process quality characteristics. Analysis of variance (ANOVA) technique
was employed to check the adequacy and signiﬁcance of mathematical models. Moreover,
the optimal setting of process parameters was determined. A conﬁrmation test was also
conducted in order to verify the developed models and the optimal settings. The results
show that Q-optimal design is a very promising method in FDM process parameter opti-
mization. The results also conﬁrm the adequacy of the developed models.

1. Introduction
Rapid manufacturing technology is the additive fabrication technique used for manufacturing solid objects in a layer-
by-layer procedure directly from a CAD ﬁle (Computer Aided Design). Rapid manufacturing, also known as additive
manufacturing, is an invention meant to deal with the increased competition in the global economy that has resulted in
the need for manufacturers to deliver new customized products faster than before in order to meet customer demands
[1] . It essentially shortens the design as well as the production cycle, which facilitates the process of revolutionizing the
traditional manufacturing procedures without compromising the functionality and mechanical performance of processed
part. There are various additive manufacturing techniques, which include fused deposition modeling (FDM), direct metal

techniques differ in terms of manufacturing styles and types of material those could be used to produce the parts.
The FDM process was introduced commercially to the trade in 1990s as a concept modeling by Stratasys Inc. It is an ad-
ditive manufacturing technology that is used for a wide array of functions including prototyping, modeling, and production
applications [2] . This technology has the capability to create 3D shapes without tooling [3] . Fig. 1 shows the schematic il-
lustration of the FDM process. In FDM process, the feedstock ﬁlament model material is heated and melted to a semi-liquid
state. The semi-liquid material is then extruded through the tip of the nozzle head. The nozzle head moves vertically and
horizontally to draw the 3D object directly from a digital data in a layer by layer style. A variety of thermoplastic materials
are supported by FDM process, such as ABS, Nylon 12, polycarbonate (PC), and PC-ABS blend [3,4] .
The quality of the manufactured products by 3D printing technologies such as FDM is a crucial factor that determines a
company’s success within the industry because it sets the appearance, properties, service life, and cost of the ﬁnal product.
Reduction of build time (manufacturing time) and feedstock material consumption without compromising the mechanical
performance is the major concern in most industrial applications affecting the manufacturing cost and the functionality of
the processed products. Build time can be deﬁned as the amount of time required by the modeler to build the real part
on the FDM platform. It is a function of many variables. Compared to other 3D printing processes, the build time in FDM
process is considerably dependent on many factors. In general, the FDM is a slower process compared to other 3D printing
process such as SLS and SLA from build time point of view [2,5] . Feedstock material consumption is the amount of mate-
rial used by the system for building a physical part. Unlike other additive manufacturing processes, such as SLS and SLA,
feedstock material consumption and the material deposition rate in FDM are the major factors in reducing build time. The
material deposition rate is mainly dependent on some building conditions such as layer thickness and road width. While
the mechanical properties of processed parts by FDM are lower than the part manufactured by traditional manufacturing
processes. One way to decrease the build time and feedstock material consumption and improve the mechanical perfor-
mance is through the appropriate adjustments in FDM process parameters to be set for the part. Therefore, the knowledge
of the optimum process settings is an important issue in the production and the quality control in the FDM manufacturing
process.
Several studies have been undertaken to examine the impact of varying FDM process parameters to optimize a qual-
ity characteristic such as mechanical property, build time, dimensional stability, and surface ﬁnish of the fabricated parts
through proper process parameter setting, but the progress has been slow because of the complex nature of the FDM pro-
cess and conﬂicting process parameters for this process. Thrimurthulu et al. [6] established empirical models for estimating
the optimum build orientation for surface roughness and build time. However, the models could not be used to predict
other process parameters. Nancharaiah et al. [7] used statistical designs to examine the impact of process conditions on
dimensional accuracy. They concluded that the slice height and raster to raster air gap are the most inﬂuential factors for
the accuracy of FDM built parts. However, the optimal process settings were not determined. Masood et al . [8] evaluated
the impact of the FDM operating conditions on the tensile strength of FDM polycarbonate manufactured parts. The study
concluded that the maximum part strength was achieved with 0.6064 mm of bead width and 45 ° of raster angle with using
build style as solid normal. In a more recent study, Sahu et al . [9] investigated the impact of fabrication conditions on the
accuracy of the manufactured prototype using Mamdani fuzzy model. This study revealed that the percentage difference in
dimension between the estimated values and the actual values using the optimized settings was less than 4.5%. However,
this approach requires developing rules and database. Thus, it needs high level of expertise knowledge and experience. Ku-
mar and Regalla [10] used factorial design to investigate the effect of slice height, raster pattern, part direction, road width
and contour width on build time and support material usage. It was found that the slice height and part direction play a

From the extensive literature review, it can be revealed that many attempts [6–9,12] have been made to study the im-
pact of some FDM operating parameters on the build quality, mechanical characteristics, surface roughness, and dimensional
accuracy. Nevertheless, there are still some obvious shortcomings in the literature. Firstly, from the observations of the re-
viewed studies, it can be noticed that the selection of FDM processing parameters to be optimized is totally based on op-
timizing one quality characteristic at a time. Secondly, a single optimal setting of process variables was determined for one
quality characteristic, but in real life applications, the same optimal setting of processing parameters can yield undesirable
results for another quality characteristic. Hence, it is important that the manufactured part must withhold more than one
quality characteristic. Thirdly, it can also be noted that very little work has been done to develop comprehensive mathemat-
ical models that could relate the build time, material consumption [10,11] and dynamic mechanical properties (i.e., dynamic
ﬂexural strength) with all critical process parameters, particularly when the process is subject to many constraints. This
may include irregular experimental regions due to restrictions applied on the level of factor and size of the design. It is
diﬃcult to obtain a complete picture on the relationship between process parameters and these quality characteristics using
standard experimental designs, such as Taguchi design and full factorial design, especially if the problem requires a sec-
ond order response surface model when some combinations of the levels of factors are not feasible and/or are irregular in
practical application. Finally, if the goal is to reduce both build time and material consumption while maximizing dynamic
mechanical properties, the previous studies did not provide a conclusive solution to such problems.
To overcome above research gaps, this study contributes to this literature in several ways. Unlike previous studies which
focus on optimizing one quality characteristic at a time, this study investigates the effect of six processing parameters on
three quality characteristics, namely build time, material consumption and mechanical performance of the manufactured
parts under dynamic and cyclic conditions. In this regard, this study presents analytic and graphical techniques for estima-
tion and optimization of FDM processing parameters using Q-optimal design and graphical optimization technique. These
techniques are constructed by implementing computer algorithms that optimize some design-related criteria. The optimized
parameter settings will have minimum bias. This study also aims to determine the optimal process parameters by devel-
oping mathematical models that optimize build time and material consumption simultaneously without compromising the
dynamic mechanical properties. The optimal process setting is validated using conﬁrmation experiments.
2. Q-optimal response surface design
Optimal designs are a class of experimental designs generated by a computer algorithm based on a speciﬁc statistical
criterion and are usually ’optimal’ to estimate the process parameters without bias and with minimum prediction error [13] .
There are many types of optimality criteria [14] :
• A-Optimality seeks to provide the minimum generalized variance of the parameter estimates of the regression coeﬃ-
cients.
• G-Optimality seeks to reduce the maximum average integral of the prediction variance over the selected model.
• D-Optimality chooses runs that reduce the determinant of the (X ′ X) −1 variance–covariance matrix and maximize infor-
mation about the parameter coeﬃcients. It is used to create factorial experiments to ﬁnd vital factors to the process.
• Q-Optimality seeks to provide minimum integrated prediction variance under the fraction of design space (FDS) curve.
The Q-Optimal design (also called IV-Optimal design or V-Optimal design) is recommended to establish RSM when the
objective is to ﬁnd the optimum parameter settings and building mathematical models with better accuracy in the predicted
response [15] . This type of computer-aided designs is particularly useful to reduce the number of runs, and to use irregularly
shaped experimental design and to exclude a speciﬁc area from design space where responses cannot be measured due
to the physical constraints applied on the parameter settings and the region of operability, such as in FDM process [16] .
Furthermore, the Q-optimal design is also used to ﬁt higher empirical models, for instance cubic, quartic, ﬁfth and sixth
models or to ﬁt a custom empirical model.

3.2.2. Developing the experimental design matrix
Response surface methodology (RSM) is an experimental technique often used to analyze and to solve problems by ex-
ploring relationships between the predictors and the responses to identify the variables that have signiﬁcant inﬂuences on
the product quality and its properties. It also aims for process parameter optimization to determine the optimal settings
of the variables within the region of interest. The precision of parameter prediction and estimation highly depends on the
accurate planning and careful execution of the experimental study as well as on the accuracy of the measure of outcome.
To study the impact of processing parameters on build time, material consumption and dynamic ﬂexural modulus, the ex-
perimental design was carried out in accordance with Q-optimal design, based on six factors at different levels having 38
runs. The experimental design matrix was augmented with extra unique model points (22 runs) in order to estimate the
variability associated with the measured responses, to minimize the residual (or pure) error, to carry out the lack-of-ﬁt test,
to minimum high leverage points in order to develop adequate mathematical models. The JMP, Minitab and Design Expert
software were used to develop the experimental plan and to analyze all statistical data collected from experimentation. The
experimental conditions and the result obtained for each experimental run as shown in Table 3 were analyzed and evaluated
to develop the best ﬁtted empirical models.

4.1. Sensitivity analysis of ﬁtting model in relation to the experimental data
The objective of design sensitivity analysis is to determine which model can be used to ﬁt the experimental data appro-

5.1. Inﬂuence of process variables on build time and feedstock material consumption
Based on the mathematical models given by Eqs. (4 and 5) , the study of the effects of various process parameters has
been made in order to analyze the optimal operating conditions that can be used for achieving minimum build time and
feedstock material consumption. The main effects of FDM parameters on various responses are presented through pertur-
bation plot and 3D graphs. The perturbation plot allows studying and comparing the main effect of all parameters at a
particular design point. The perturbation graph does not show the interaction effects. Therefore the interaction and 3D
response surface plots for the most signiﬁcant parameters are used to study the effect of parameters interactions.
The perturbation plots for build time and feedstock material consumption with respect to the six variables are shown
in Fig. 7 (a and b). The steep slope of some process variables obtained shows that the output responses are sensitive to
those process parameters and a ﬂat line demonstrates insensitivity to modiﬁcation of that variable. Layer thickness (A) has
a strong impact on build time and feedstock material consumption, as can be observed from Fig. 7 (a and b). As the layer
thickness increases, a dramatic decrease in build time and feedstock material consumption can be seen. This is due to the
high value of layer thickness resulting in minimum number of layers and minimum number of passes required by the nozzle
to build up the part, which helps in reducing the time of cleaning the nozzle after deposition of every layer, and thereby
reducing the build time and feedstock material consumption. Fig 7 (a and b) shows that the air gap (B) has a marginal effect
on build time, but it has a signiﬁcant effect on feedstock material consumption. No air gap between adjacent rasters means
that the toolpaths are closer to each other. This will require more time to ﬁll the part. Larger values of air gap, such as
0.4 mm, will leave gaps between the rasters. This can speed up build time and use less feedstock material consumption by
reducing the number of deposited layers with minimum number of passes for extrusion head.
Raster angle (C) shows a marginal effect on build time as shown in Fig. 7 a. However, it has no inﬂuence on feedstock
material consumption, and thus it was eliminated from the regression model. A marginal increase in build time can be seen
( Fig. 7 a) as the raster angle increases up to center level (45 °). However, by increasing in raster angle beyond 45 ° up to 90 °,
the build time also starts decreasing. A possible explanation is that the use of the lowest value (0 °) or the highest value
(90 °) of raster angle results in smaller length of rasters and shorter cycle lengths by the nozzle head, and therefore reduces
the build time required for manufacturing the part.

Air gap has a strong impact on the ﬂexural modulus of the printed parts as can be seen in Fig. 12 . From this ﬁgure, it
can be seen that with an increase in the raster to raster air gap from 0.00 mm to 0.5 mm, a signiﬁcant decrease occurred in

each other resulting in dense structure and high quality of bonding strength between the interlayers. However, the penalty
of using lower value of air gap is the increase in build time and feedstock material consumption. Thus, it is very critical to
determine the optimal setting for this factor to achieve a balance between mechanical properties, build time and feedstock
material consumption.
Fig. 12 shows that the ﬂexural strength of the FDM manufactured parts is slightly affected by the modiﬁcation of raster
angle, but it helps in obtaining better properties of the parts. It is observed that as the raster angle increases from 0 ° to 45 °
there is a marginal reduction in the ﬂexural modulus and then it increases on further increase in raster angle behind 45 °
up to 90 °, but faintly. One compelling argument for this description is that the number of raster required to build the part
is reduced with the lower value of raster angle, which therefore helps in decreasing the chances of incomplete ﬁlling in the
part. To articulate it more clearly, Fig. 13 shows two examples of the sample subjected to the tension load. One sample is
fabricated with 0 ° raster angle and the second specimen is fabricated with 90 ° raster angle. It can be seen from Fig. 13 that
the applied load requires the separation and breakage of a large numbers of molecule bonds and individual plastic ﬁbers.
Thus the part with a raster angle of 0 ° demonstrates higher dynamic ﬂexural modulus. However, when the sample is printed
with 90 ° as shown in Fig. 13 , the separation and breakage between rasters occur at the interface of the deposited rasters,
which tends to be more degradable and weaker.
In the Fig. 12 , it is observed that the build orientation also has a little impact on the ﬂexural modulus of the parts.
But the minimum value of the build direction (0 °) helps to avoid stair-stepping imperfection effect. This eliminates the
chances of occurring voids and incomplete ﬁlling, and thus the manufactured part becoming dense in interior structure.
Fig. 12 clearly shows that the highest ﬂexural modulus can be obtained with 0.5177 mm road width. This is because a
road width of 0.5177 mm provides a ﬁner width of rasters which helps to create better structure and reduces non-uniform
thermal gradients, which help to reduce distortion leading to high quality of bonding strength between interlayer.

6. Multi-objective optimization through desirability function and overlay plot
Graphical optimization through desirability function (DF) is a widely used approach in industry for simultaneous opti-
mization in multiple criteria problems, when the objective is to ﬁnd out a set of parameter settings that optimize all the
experimental responses for a speciﬁc system. Desirability function enables balancing among all experimental responses with
the additional beneﬁt that the results can be plotted. It has advantages over many other multi-criteria optimization tech-
niques such as linear programming. FDM generally has a complex build and physical mechanism that exhibits complicated
problem to determine the optimal operating conditions to achieve the highest product quality. Due to multiple and con-
ﬂicting objectives and interaction effect of the process parameters, FDM is a challenging manufacturing process in terms of
determining the optimal process parameters and developing robust industrial applications . It is needed to achieve a min-
imum build time and feedstock material consumption while maximizing the dynamic ﬂexural modulus of fabricated part.
Graphical optimization helps us to produce overlay plots, which are quite convenient for visually searching the optimum
parameter settings. Fig. 16 shows overlay graph, which has three regions. The yellow area is feasible (sweet spot), which
satisﬁes all the requirements for build time, feedstock material consumption and dynamic ﬂexural modulus simultaneously.
The gray regions on the overlay graph are undesirable areas and do not ﬁt the optimization criteria, while the red regions
on the overlay graph corresponds to the case, where the point estimate meets the optimization criteria requirements, but
part of an interval estimate is outside the interval bound.
Through the desirability approach, each response ( y i ) is converted into a single desirability ( d i ), that varies over the
scale 0 ≤d i ≤1, where d i =1 when a goal satisﬁes with the response requirement, and d i = 0 when a goal is outside the
acceptable region [20] . In this study, the desirability function ‘minimum-the-better’ was selected to minimize the build time
and feedstock material consumption using Eq. (7) , while the desirability function ‘maximum-the-better’ was selected to
maximize the dynamic ﬂexural modulus using Eq. (8) . Once each response is transformed, the individual desirabilities are
then combined into a single response using the overall desirability function, which gives the global optimum of the multi-
response optimization, as expressed in Eq. (9) [21] . The factor settings with the highest global desirability are selected to be
the optimum settings. Percentage error of prediction is determined by Eq. (10) .

6.1. Machine constraints
FDM is a multiple constrained optimization process which imposes speciﬁc settings for each parameter and which usually
affect the selection of the optimal operating conditions. One of the constraints applied on the layer thickness is FDM ma-
chine has four speciﬁc settings for layer thickness (0.1270 mm, 0.1778 mm, 0.2540 mm and 0.3302 mm). This is because layer
thickness is restricted and controlled by the machine nozzle diameter. Thus if the ﬁnal optimal setting for layer thickness
is not within these four speciﬁc values, the optimal parameter setting will not be feasible or possible in real-life practice.
Another practical constraint is imposed on the variable ‘number of contours’ which is restricted up to 10 contours (1 to 10).
The machine cannot create parts with fractional number of contours (e.g. 1.5 or 7.5). A solution is a set of values for the
parameters that meets all constraints. Hence, in process optimization, these constraints are taken into account in order to
ﬁnd the optimal setting for both variables to be in the feasible region. The desired target for other process variables such
as air gap, raster angle, build orientation and road width was deﬁned as ‘within the range’ and the responses, i.e., build
time and feedstock material consumption were deﬁned as ‘minimum-the-better’ ( Table 9 ) to achieve the lowest build time
and feedstock material consumption, while the dynamic ﬂexural modules was deﬁned as ‘maximum-the-better’ in order to
maximize the mechanical performance of the manufactured parts.

8. Conclusions
In this study, the mathematical models have been formulated to predict build time and feedstock material consumption
in Fused Deposition Modeling additive manufacturing. An experimental plan based on the Q-optimal design response surface
methodology has been developed and applied to perform the experimentation work and the following conclusions were
obtained:
• Based on ANOVA and F -test results, the most effective parameters on build time, feedstock material consumption and
dynamic ﬂexural modulus are found to be layer thickness, air gap, build direction and number of contours. However,
raster angle and road width are less effective on build time and feedstock material consumption.
• The increase in layer thickness (from 0.1270 mm to 0.3302 mm) and air gap (from 0 mm to 0.5 mm) with decreasing
number of contours reduces build time and feedstock material consumption signiﬁcantly.
• Dynamic ﬂexural modulus improved signiﬁcantly using thick layers, zero air gap and 10 contours.
• From the multi-objective optimization, the optimal parameter conditions obtained are layer thickness = 0.2540 mm, air
gap = 0.499 mm, raster angle = 0.0 0 0 0266 °, build orientation = 0.0 0 0 0339 °, road width = 0.484 mm and 7 outlines for min-
imizing build time and feedstock material consumption, while maximizing the dynamic ﬂexural modulus. Since the fab-
ricated part used in this study was small in size, the optimum settings would save a huge amount of time and material
in complex and big parts if they are used.
• Mathematical models formulated in this study were validated through conﬁrmation experiments and the results have
demonstrated that the models can accurately describe the correlation between the process conditions and the experi-
mental responses within 96% conﬁdence interval, as the variations between the actual values and the estimated values
lie between –0.233% and 1.604%. Clearly, this proves great value of the experimental ﬁndings and conclusions. The de-
veloped models will help practitioners in reducing the number of experimental works by estimating the build time,
feedstock material consumption and dynamic ﬂexural modulus in relation to process parameters prior to manufacturing
the FDM parts, thereby reducing cost, time, and effort.
• The study has proved that the Q-optimal design type of response surface methodology was simple, eﬃcient, powerful,
ﬂexible, cost effective and reliable design for process optimization involving multiple variables and multiple responses.
