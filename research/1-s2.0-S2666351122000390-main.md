A B S T R A C T
One of the most popular techniques of Additive Manufacturing (AM) being used currently is Fused Deposition
Modelling (FDM). FDM is currently a nascent technology and has signiﬁcant scope for improvement, particularly
when it comes to the dimensional accuracy of the printed parts. The dimensional accuracy of the parts governs the
usability of the product. These products when used in assemblies need to have very tight tolerances. These tol-
erances play a vital role in the quality of the ﬁnished product. For FDM to become commercially viable, the ability
to predict this dimensional variation caused by different print parameters is essential, it would result in improved
quality of the fabricated product and saving of time and resources. In this research, we studied the impact of
signiﬁcant parameters on the dimensional accuracy for different geometries such as cylindrical shafts, holes and
rectangular slots. Predicting the dimensional variation is accomplished by using Decision Tree Machine Learning
Algorithm. This algorithm's result is most suitable and provides accurate predictions; furthermore, the effec-
tiveness of the model developed is validated by the R2 score of 0.67, the model can be further developed to
establish industry-friendly functionality.

for the ﬁrst time in a commercial setting. The initial kinds of machines
used light-sensitive materials (i.e. solidiﬁes upon irradiation of light) and
were part of the stereolithography family. Over the next couple of years,
another technology was gaining momentum in the rapid prototyping
domain which very quickly became one of the most popular technique for
academic and research work. The Fused Deposition Modelling (FDM)
machine was made commercially available by Stratasys in the late 1980s
and early 1990s [1].
Additive Manufacturing or 3D Printing is the process of rapidly
materialising Computer-Aided Design (CAD) models. The AM technique
could create prototypes and can almost instantly realise complex shapes
and intricate features in the design [2,3]. 3D printing has the potential to
be utilised in conjunction with 3D scanning which allows precise
modelling of parts. 3D Scanning can be employed to properly and efﬁ-
ciently reverse engineer complex and special details in highly functional
technologies. The two techniques (Additive Manufacturing and 3D

2. Literature review
2.1. Computer-aided design (CAD)
The process of Additive Manufacturing (AM) or 3D printing is
fundamentally different from the conventional method of subtractive
manufacturing techniques. The FDM printers use the CAD models from
the computers to fabricate the end products. CAD Modelling is the use of
computer software to manifest new designs for engineers and re-
searchers. The FDM process is nothing but a mere 2D layer stacking (i.e
stacked on the z-axis) with different parameters as selected by the user.
The layer stacking process in FDM helps designers to produce designs
with intricate features and structures in reduced time. The built-in
tessellation of FDM machines creates a basic boundary which uses tri-
angles to cover the entire surface of the solid design.
2.2. Standard Tessellation Language (STL)
The ﬁle format that the FDM 3D printer uses for fabrication is called
the Standard Tessellation Language ﬁle (or STL File), which is the input
to the printer which contains information regarding the build of the part.
In the STL ﬁle, the part is deﬁned using triangles and each triangle's
vertex is arranged in such a way to satisfy the design characteristics of the
parts. This triangular mesh creation is considered to be the best suited for
AM applications, further, the STL format is the industry standard for all
3D Printing applications [16].
2.3. Fused Deposition Modelling (FDM)
The Fused Deposition Modelling was developed by Stratasys in the
early 1990s and has gained signiﬁcant positive growth and momentum in
recent years. FDM 3D printing falls under the material extrusion category
of additive manufacturing. The simplistic nature of the technology en-
ables it to produce rapid and working prototypes with ease. The FDM
Machines are easy to operate, handle and maintain therefore allowing the
production process in a simple home/ofﬁce environment, hence, these
properties of FDM make it highly popular for hobbyists, engineers, stu-
dents and researchers alike.
This material extrusion technique uses the material in either a pellet
or ﬁlament form factor. The material is heated close to melting point and
then fed through a set of rollers. As the material starts to ﬂow, the nozzle
steadily moves around the build plate or work bed tracing the design to
layer by layer stack (or deposit) the material and form the end product
[17–19].
2.4. Thermoplastic materials
2.4.1. Acrylonitrile butadiene styrene
Commonly referred to as ABS is the most widely and popularly used
thermoplastic for the FDM 3D Printing Process. It is an opaque thermo-
plastic that possesses properties which lead to convenient printing
although the characteristics (dimensional stability and mechanical
properties) of the printed parts are predominantly dependent on the
various print parameters settings available in the FDM 3D Printer. ABS
being amorphous has no exact melting point though, it is close to 230 C.
It is durable, economical and can be thermoformed to almost any shape
as required by the designer.
However, ABS does possess some limitations to its use such as low
strength, limited ability to ﬂex or bend, and warps upon itself easily,
therefore it necessitates the use of a heated work table for optimum
printing and processing. The fumes emitted at the time of the printing
process can also be dangerous for humans [19,20].

and has a signiﬁcantly lower tendency to warp, so there's no need for a
hot work table bed when using PLA. It is a biodegradable plastic, which
means it's good for the environment, however, this causes PLA to absorb

2.5. FDM process parameters
FDM 3D Printing machines offer ﬂexibility in the parts produced
along with good part quality. As the technology has evolved over the
years the process has increased efﬁciency, productivity, reduced cost as
well as lead times. In order to comply with the varying application needs
the process parameter or print settings can be tuned to suit each
requirement. The entire process of FDM (and Additive Manufacturing in
general) is governed and affected by the combination of these parame-
ters. In general, these can be categorised as i) process-related, ii) struc-
tural characteristics and iii) extruder properties. Selecting the most
optimum parameters is quintessential in determining the part quality,
lead and process time, material usage, dimensional accuracy and cost of
the printing process. The process parameters taken into consideration for
this research have been described as follows:
- Wall Thickness: It is the distance between the outer surface of the
part and the opposite inner sheer surface of the printed design.
- Inﬁll Density: A part printed using the FDM 3D Printer is not
completely solid, it is ﬁlled sparsely with the material used for the
process. Therefore, inﬁll density refers to the percentage of volume
ﬁlled with material of the total available volume of the part.
- Build Plate Temperature: As mentioned above, the ABS material has
a tendency to wrap upon itself when cooled rapidly so to avoid such
occurrences in the printed product the work bed or the build plate is
heated to a temperature which facilitates gradual and steady cooling
of the product. The temperature at which the build plate is main-
tained is called the build plate temperature.
- Print Speed: The speed of depositing the material onto the work bed
as the extruder navigates on the XY axes to build the part layer by
layer.
- Layer Thickness: The height of the material that is deposited from the
extruder or the nozzle measured along the Z-axis from the work bed.
- Extrusion Temperature: It is the temperature at which the material is
heated in the nozzle to melt the ﬁlament before it is deposited on the
work bed [22–25].
Based on research conducted previously, It has been determined that
the following print parameters have the greatest effect on dimensional
accuracy, in ascending order: inﬁll density, layer thickness, print speed,
extrusion temperature, and build plate temperature [46,47].
2.6. Machine Learning(ML)
ML is a branch of Artiﬁcial Intelligence that allows machines to learn
without ﬁrst being programmed to do so; it is a method for achieving
Artiﬁcial Intelligence (AI) in computer systems. With advances in com-
puter technology, we now have the ability to store and process large
amounts of data, owing to this, machine learning in the past few years has
gained immense popularity. With the help of ML a computer can identify
and learn complex patterns in huge sets of data and using this knowledge
it can be used to give predictions on new datasets, this feature of ML
offers countless solutions to many intricate problems [35,36].
In order to make these predictions on a new set of readings, the ML
model needs to ﬁrst train on a subset of data available, this is known as
the training set; and the process altogether is known as the learning stage.
The learning stage has different approaches and can be broadly classiﬁed
into two groups, supervised and unsupervised learning. In supervised
learning, the original values of all attributes (dependent or independent)
are fed into the model, which then works on reducing the errors in
predicted values from actual values. Supervised learning can be further
classiﬁed into classiﬁcation techniques and regression techniques. In the
classiﬁcation technique, the variable to be predicted is categorical and in
the regression technique, the variable to be predicted is continuous and
numerical. In unsupervised learning, the data is not labelled nor organ-
ised. The model has to act on the given data without any previous
learning unlike supervised learning. The model's task here is to sort the
data and group it based on similarities, patterns or differences [37,38,43,
44].
2.6.1. Decision tree regression
A Decision Tree is a representation of all possible decision paths in the
form of a tree, these paths are inferred from historic data on which the
model is trained. A decision tree is built by recursive partitioning or
splitting the training data into distinct points called nodes. Each node is
followed by an internal node that is a test condition, the internal node is
split on the basis of the test result into branches, and this process keeps
recurring until we are left with nodes containing pure data also known as
leaf nodes, in regression these leaf nodes assign values to new parameters
which are to be predicted. Recursive partitioning is used to minimise the
degree of impurity at each stage for each node. Here impurity refers to
the randomness in the data [39].
The nodes or the dataset is split at numerous points, the division is
achieved by the algorithm as it aims to minimise the ﬁtness function. The
ﬁtness function can be understood as the difference between the actual
value and the value predicted by the model. The value corresponding to
the split having the lowest ﬁtness is chosen to be the split point; it is a
recursive process. Decision trees essentially use the fast divide and
conquer greedy algorithm that splits the data into smaller parts in iter-
ation [40].
The technique can be used to form trees for predicting both cate-
gorical and numerical valued data points, therefore they can be used in
both regression and classiﬁcation. This algorithm is chosen over the
others as it offers ﬂexibility to handle a broad range of data, the structure
is simple to understand and evaluate, and statistical tests are performed
with ease for validation purposes. Therefore, it can be said that it is a
reliable algorithm to use and apply for the scope of this research [41].
3. Experimental details and procedure
The various use case scenarios that the FDM printing technique offers
any average user to print and fabricate multiple forms and shapes of
geometries. Therefore, the variation in the dimensions of every part de-
pends on the geometry that is being manufactured. In order to account
for as many possible geometries in the printed part, a comprehensive
CAD Model was meticulously designed which encompassed the shapes
such as shafts, holes and rectangular slots. The use of multiple geometries
in the same part allowed for minimum use of material while having
maximum data for the machine learning model. The part drawing of the
specimen used is shown in Fig. 1.
3.1. RSM based experimentation
The Response Surface Methodology was used to design the sample
space on the Design Expert Software. For both the material (ABS and
PLA) a face - centred small resolution CCD generated the runs with
varying combination of print parameters which enables the Machine
Learning model to identify the trends and predict using the same data.
For the ABS material there were six (6) parameters studied and for PLA as
a print material ﬁve (5) print parameters are taken into consideration.
The response studied in both is percentage dimensional variation. All of
the other process parameters apart from those in the Tables 2 and 3 were
kept constant through the various runs. The STL ﬁles were created in
correspondence to the RSM generated print parameters combination for
ABS and PLA, the ﬁles were then fed into the printing machine for each
run. The experiment results for both are given Tables 4 and 5.

4.2. Analysis of dimensional variation in different geometries
Based on the data recorded for both ABS and PLA it was observed that
the outer diameter of the shafts had the lowest average deviation,
whereas the inner diameter reﬂected the highest deviation from the
desired value, one possible explanation for this is that the inner area of
shafts cools slower than the outside which makes the material more
ﬂowable inside than outside. This is also conﬁrmed by the fact that the
holes and rectangular slots also show huge dimensional variation
[26–30].
On comparison of the parameters for ABS and PLA and their corre-
sponding percentage deviation it was observed that one particular
parameter - Wall thickness had different effects on different materials. In
Fig. 4 this relation is illustrated. At 0.8 mm wall thickness, the deviation
is 4.9% and 8.02% in ABS and PLA respectively, at 1.6 mm Wall thick-
ness, the deviation is 6.4% and 5.52% in ABS and PLA respectively.
Hence it can be clearly inferred that increasing the value of Wall thick-
ness would result in a higher deviation in ABS and on the other hand
lower deviation in PLA on the other hand [31].

5. Conclusion
The ever-increasing demand for customization, sustainability and
improved quality has brought additive manufacturing techniques to the
forefront in the production domain. The prediction model for deviation
of various shapes enables superior allowances and tolerances during the
product design phase that allows engineers and designers to evaluate the
product before the manufacturing phase itself.
On the basis of observed results from the present study, it can be
deduced that there exists an appreciable amount of deviation in the parts
produced using the FDM technique. The overall average percentage de-
viation observed in the data set is around 6%. It is also worth noting that
ABS parts have a signiﬁcantly lower deviation than PLA parts. Addi-
tionally, it is seen that the inner diameter of the shaft exhibits the
maximum deviation, whereas the outer diameter displays the lowest
deviation, this trend remains similar for both the materials used in this
study. The effect of wall thickness was also studied for ABS and PLA, it is
astonishing to observe that with increasing wall thickness deviation in-
creases for ABS, on the other hand, it reduces for PLA. Subsequently, the
machine learning model developed using the supervised learning tech-
nique predicts deviation in parts based on print parameters as input with
satisfactory results. The machine learning model works on the decision-
tree algorithm and ﬁnds patterns in the effect of the various parameters
studied in this research on dimensional accuracy.
6. Scope of future improvement
In future, the scope of the model can be expanded by considering
more materials, increasing the print parameters taken into consideration,
and using other 3D printing techniques apart from FDM. Additionally,
the machine learning model is currently deployed locally, however, it can
also be deployed on servers to gain access from anywhere and from any
device which increases the model's end-user and industrial applications.
The predictive model will facilitate the academic and industrial growth
of additive manufacturing at an increased pace to match the market's
competitive attributes [42].
