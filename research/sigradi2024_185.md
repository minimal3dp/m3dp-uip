The Effect of 3D Printers on the Form
Manufacturing Process in Spatial Design with
Biomimicry Approach
Rana Uzun¹, Ömer Faruk Uzun²
¹Gebze Technical University, Kocaeli, Türkiye,
²Sakarya University of Applied Sciences, Sakarya, Türkiye


rkamiloglu@gtu.edu.tr; omerfarukuzun@subu.edu.tr
Abstract. In today's architectural sectors, 3D printing technique stands out with its
sustainable,
ecological,
and
economic
advantages
compared
to
traditional
manufacturing methods. This research experimentally examines the impact of 3D
printers on producing spatial forms designed with a biomimicry approach. The stingray
form was selected as the primary material, its physical properties were analyzed, and its
algorithm was created using parametric modeling. The stingray form was 3D modeled
and its prototypes were produced at a 1/100 scale using a home 3D printer. The
prototypes were evaluated based on six additive manufacturing criteria. The results
show that biomimicry forms can be produced in 3D printers with a high degree of fidelity
to the original. This method offers a sustainable, ecological, and economic approach by
saving time and materials and reducing labor costs.
Keywords: Digital fabrication, Biomimicry, Parametric design, Additive manufacture, 3D
printers
1
Introduction
Biomimicry, a practice that seeks innovative solutions by emulating nature's
3.8-billion-year-old research and development process, was first introduced by
Janine Benyus in 1997. This approach involves studying the functions of
organisms, ecosystems, and natural processes to apply this knowledge to
various fields such as engineering, architecture, and design (Benyus, 1997). By
mimicking nature's principles of sustainability and efficiency, biomimicry offers
significant environmental and economic benefits.
The intersection of biomimicry and design involves integrating natural
mechanisms into human-made creations, enhancing both aesthetics and

(Pawlyn, 2011). By studying natural forms, structures, and processes,

1.1
Biomimicry as an Approach to Spatial Design
Biomimicry offers innovative and sustainable solutions for both indoor and
outdoor designs. In interior spaces, it combines aesthetic appeal with
functionality through forms and structures inspired by nature. For instance, the
spiral structure of the Nautilus can create visually and acoustically engaging
spaces like museums, while the aerodynamic shape of whale fins can reduce
wind resistance in interior designs (Pawlyn, 2011).
In outdoor designs, biomimicry integrates functional, stable, and
aesthetically pleasing aspects of natural systems. The hexagonal cell structure
of beehives, for example, can optimize space utilization and structural stability
in parks and gardens (Vincent et al., 2006). Similarly, the expansive surface
area of banana leaves can be used to create shaded areas, and the robust
composition of spider webs can inspire canopy designs (Hensel et al., 2010).
Applying biomimicry principles to space design enhances aesthetic appeal,
functionality, and sustainability, creating environments that harmonize with
nature.
1.2
Sustainable Design and Manufacturing Strategies with Biomimicry
and Additive Manufacturing Technologies
Integrating biomimicry into design and production processes supports
sustainability goals for both traditional and digital production methods.
Combining nature-inspired designs with additive manufacturing technologies
not only offers aesthetic and functional benefits but also minimizes
environmental impact (Pawlyn, 2011). This synergy between biomimicry and
additive manufacturing holds significant potential for developing future
sustainable design and manufacturing strategies.
While subtractive manufacturing generates high material waste and energy
consumption, additive manufacturing is a more efficient and sustainable
production method. It uses only the necessary materials, minimizing waste and
reducing energy consumption, thereby promoting the sustainable use of natural
resources (Bogue, 2013).
Additive manufacturing (3D printing) in construction uses various materials
beyond traditional concrete. For example, plastic composites, valued for their
durability and lightweight, were used by Eindhoven University of Technology to
produce a 3D-printed house (Van Rijswijk, 2017). Metal 3D printing was
employed for a large office building in Dubai, completed by Gensler in 2019
(Gensler, 2019). Earth and clay offer sustainable options, as seen in the TECLA

2
Methodology
The study methodology comprises four stages: sample selection, parametric
modelling, additive manufacturing and evaluation. The following paragraphs
provide an explanation of the stages. In the initial stage of the study, a detailed
literature review was conducted to identify forms that can be used in spatial
design based on the principle of biomimicry. The stingray fish form was selected
as the main material of the study, given its multifunctional spatial usage features
that facilitate the creation of open, semi-open, and closed spaces. In the second
stage, the previously selected form was modelled parametrically using Rhino 8,
Grasshopper tool and add-ons such as Human and Pufferfish. Two different
variations of the forms were produced using parametric algorithms.  In the third
stage, two different variations of the 3D model were exported in .stl format.
These models were imported in OrcaSlicer 3D slicing program and .gcode files
were generated. The slicing settings used in OrcaSlicer program to print
prototypes on a 3D printer are given in Table 1.

3.1
Selection of the Sampling Form
In the study, a variety of form types observed in nature were examined through
a literature review of the biomimicry design approach. As outlined in the
methodology, factors such as the potential for spatial use in different functions
(open, semi-open and closed areas) and the ability of the form structure to
accommodate canopy, wall and floor features simultaneously were considered.
Based on these criteria, the stingray form was selected as the material for the
study. The stingray form has been adapted to create two distinct variations of a
space form through the biomimicry approach. The diagrammatic representation
of this transformation is provided in Figure 2. Potential applications of the two

3.4
Evaluation of 3D Printer Production
The two prototypes were evaluated in accordance with the six additive
manufacturing criteria set forth in the methodology. The evaluation results are
presented as follows:
(1) Visual comparison: Upon examination of the prototypes in terms of
visual comparison criterion, it was observed that the printed prototypes of the
v.1 and v.2 forms exhibited a high degree of visual compatibility with the 3D
model. However, it was noted that some production errors had occurred.
(2) Dimensional accuracy: The forms were measured with precision using
calipers, as shown in Figure 10. The values indicated by A, B, and C represent
the length, while the values indicated by x, y, and z represent the thickness.
The results of the measurements taken of the prototypes are compared with
the measurements taken of the 3D model and are given in Table 2, along with
the margins of error. Accordingly, the average error rates for the form v.1
prototype in terms of length and thickness were calculated as 0.8% and 17%,
respectively. The average error rates for the length and thickness of the other
prototype, form v.2, were determined as 0.4% and 16%, respectively. On the
other hand, the weight of the filament to be spent calculated in the OrcaSlicer
slicing program was compared with the weights of the produced prototypes
measured on a precision scale. Accordingly, the weight error margins of forms
v.1 and v.2 were measured as 3.56% and 3.38%, respectively (Table 2).

(3) Printing time: As a result of the slicing done in the OrcaSlicer program
used to produce form prototypes, the predicted printing time for forms v.1 and
v.2 is 107 and 106 minutes while the printing time of the actual production is
122 and 129 minutes, respectively. This differences in time may be due to
optimization problems between the slicing program and the 3D printer.
(4) Material consumption and waste: While producing on a 3D printer,
additional supports (brim, support, etc.) are created depending on the model so
that the model can stand, print correctly in parts where the model is
disconnected from the ground, and print angled parts. These supports ensure
that production takes place correctly. In addition, the 3D printer consumes
filament for operations such as cleaning its nozzle (skirt) before starting
production. Although these are essential operations for the production to run
smoothly, they also create losses in production. Filament consumption and
losses of the two prototypes produced in the study are given in Table 3. The
total filament consumption was measured as 63.28 g and 59.36 g for prototype
v.1 and v.2, respectively. The total material loss rate of production is 2.07% and
2.34% for prototype v.1 and v.2, respectively. This shows that the desired form
can be achieved with very little material loss with additive manufacturing and
3D printing technology.
(5) Support requirement: To print the prototypes as seen in Figure 7, a
very small amount of support material was needed for both forms. The total
filament used in the printing of the form v.1 prototype is 63.28 grams, with the
support accounting for 1.15 grams (1.82%) of this quantity. In comparison, the
total filament used in the printing of the form v.2 prototype is 59.36 grams, with
the support accounting for 1.22 grams (2.05%) of this quantity. The construction
of these forms using traditional production methods would necessitate the use
of a considerable quantity of formwork and scaffold material, resulting in a
significant level of material wastage. However, the utilization of 3D printing, a
form of additive manufacturing, enables a sustainable production approach
through the reduction of waste and the minimization of support material, in
comparison to traditional production methods.

4
Conclusion
The objective of this study was to investigate the impact of three-dimensional
printers, a representative example of additive manufacturing technologies, on
the generation of space forms designed with a biomimicry approach through an
experimental methodology. In this context, the form of the stingray fish was
selected as the material of study through literature research on the biomimicry
design approach. The physical properties of the selected forms were studied,
and an algorithm was generated with parametric modeling. According to the
algorithm, the stingray form was modelled in 3D in two different variations,
taking into account their potential use in spatial design. Subsequently, 1/100
scale prototypes of the two forms were produced on a home-type 3D printer.
These prototypes were evaluated using six additive manufacturing criteria: (1)
visual comparison, (2) dimensional accuracy, (3) printing time, (4) material
consumption and waste, (5) support requirements, and (6) surface defects and
quality. The main findings of the evaluation are presented below:

•
According to the visual comparison criterion, the 3D-printed prototypes
are highly compatible in form with the 3D models.
•
According to the dimensional accuracy criterion, the accuracy of the
prototypes in terms of length, thickness and weight is high and the
margin of error is very low.
•
According to the printing time criterion, the printing times of the
prototypes are close to the time predicted in the slicing program used.
•
According to the material consumption and waste criterion, waste
material, such as the brim, skirt and supports required to produce the
prototype, represents a very small proportion of the total material and
the overall material loss is very low.
•
According to the support requirement criterion, a very small amount of
support material is required to produce prototypes compared to
traditional methods.
•
According to the surface defects and quality criteria, the surfaces are
generally printed in good quality and the defects on the surfaces are
low in prototype production.

The results highlight the advantages of additive manufacturing over
traditional production methods. This method allows production with a more
sustainable, ecological and economical approach, with advantages such as the
ability to produce even complex and organic shapes inspired by biomimicry in
high quality and very close to the original, saving time and materials and
reducing labor costs. The spatial design proposal successfully produced in this
study proves that biomimicry forms, such as the stingray, can be produced very
close to the original on a 3D printer using additive manufacturing.
The fact that the Creality K1 home printer used in the study can only produce
certain sizes and types of material meant that the shapes and structures to be
used in construction had to be evaluated on 1/100 scale prototypes, limiting the
