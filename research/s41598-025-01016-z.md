Multi objective optimization of
FDM 3D printing parameters set via
design of experiments and machine
learning algorithms
Antonio Panico1,2, Alberto Corvi1,2, Luca Collini1 & Corrado Sciancalepore1
The choice of the optimal printing setup for Fused Deposition Modeling (FDM) 3D-printing technology
is challenging due to complex interactions between process parameters and mechanical properties.
This especially affects engineering applications where the maximum performance is required. To
address this challenge, this study explores the influence of main controllable printing parameters
including layer thickness, extrusion temperature, printing speed and deposition patterns, on the
mechanical properties of FDM-printed ABS specimens using the Design-of-Experiments (DoE)
approach by a 34 full factorial design. Main-effects and Interaction-effects on tensile strength, elastic
modulus, and strain at maximum stress are investigated via ANOVA analysis, providing interesting
hints to evaluate at the design stage. Given the complexity of these effects, a deeper investigation is
conducted with a quadratic regression model of the Response Surface Method and the Random Forest
regressor, with the latter enhancing the predictive capability (R2) on test data by more than 40% for
all the mechanical properties. Eventually, a Genetic Algorithm (NSGA-II) is integrated to estimate the
optimal parameter set for multiple responses. Overall results indicate that the deposition strategy is
the parameter affecting the most the overall mechanical response, with “Lines” pattern providing the
best balanced results in maximizing the elastic modulus and the tensile strength, respectively 1381
MPa and 33.3 MPa. Testing of a set of specimens printed with the found optimal parameters confirm
the model’s prediction.
Keywords  Additive Manufacturing, Design-of-Experiments (DoE), Random Forest, NSGA-II
Additive manufacturing (AM) is definitely one of the most promising technologies of the last decades, creating
a new paradigm of construction where material is deposited layer by layer sequentially to create the desired
product, in contrast with traditional subtractive or formative processes, enabling on-demand production of
customized products and reducing development time associated with mass production. Thanks to its low-cost
and no need of a controlled environment, Fused Deposition Modeling (FDM) is considered one of the most
advantageous techniques among AM technologies1,2, being suitable for a wide range of applications across
various engineering disciplines and industrial sectors, from rapid prototyping to the production of functional
components. Enabling the design of parts with complex geometries, FDM also addresses the requirements for
novel advanced applications3–7.
As well known, in this technology the material, commonly a filament of thermoplastic polymer8–10, is heated
up into a semi-melted state and then selectively extruded through a nozzle which follows the trajectory defined
in the G-Code file for the print. As a consequence, the mechanical properties of FDM-ed components are
unfortunately strongly influenced by the set of process parameters adopted for their fabrication11–13. Over the last
decade, numerous works have investigated the impact of the main controllable printing parameters such as build
orientation, raster angle14,15, infill pattern and percentage16,17, extrusion temperature18–20and layer height on the
resulting mechanical response21–23, including damage regime24,25and failure behavior26–28. In this framework, a
structured and systematic investigation on the effect of each process parameter and their mutual influences is
here addressed as a topic of interest when looking for the optimal parameters set to achieve the most suitable
properties according to the application requirements. Design-of-Experiments (DoE) is a statistical technique to
evaluate the effect of examined factors on the resulting responses by considering a set of points describing the
combinations of levels of such factors. Based on these points, a factorial design can be defined, representing the

experimental plan for the work. Many approaches to design experiments are available in literature29. The most
known is the Full Factorial Design (FFD), which is based on a factorial plan defined by the number of factors and
levels. FFD serves multiple purposes: it can be used to determine if a factor impacts the response30–33, or to apply
the Response Surface Methodology (RSM), which aims at optimization, by increasing the number of levels34.
Other works adopt the systematic approach provided by Taguchi’s method35 to set up a simplified experimental
plan in an efficient and effective way via a highly fractionated factorial design and orthogonal arrays, although
allowing to evaluate main effects and only limited interaction effects among considered parameters. An early
contributor in this field was Anitha et al.36, who studied process variables in FDM using an L18 orthogonal
array, to minimize surface roughness. Other researches37–42 employed the same approach to investigate the
impact of build orientation, raster angle, nozzle diameter, and other parameters on the tensile strength and
surface quality of PLA, ABS, and PEEK specimens. Different designs that also include the will to optimize the
responses are available in literature. Among these, Central Composite Design (CCD) and Face-centered Central
Composite Design (FCCD) are widely employed. These designs require more points than a 2k factorial design,
leading to more complex models, such as the second-order polynomial, which is able to better capture the
curvature of the response surface. This is particularly useful in RSM, where the focus is to find the optimal
values of variables to maximize or minimize the desired responses. Camargo et al.43 and Lanzotti et al.44 adopted
a coupled CCD-RSM approach to study the influence of FDM process parameters on mechanical properties in
PLA standard specimens, with the latter also including the simultaneous optimization of tensile strength and
failure strain using a commercial response optimizer. Also Borah et al.45–47 performed a similar investigation
employing machine learning techniques together with optimization algorithms to find the best combination of
parameters for mechanical responses and surface roughness. Sood et al.48,49 utilized a FCCD design to obtain
surface responses of tensile strength, flexural strength, and impact strength. Also, a comparison between RSM
and Artificial Neural Network (ANN) is presented, with the latter outperforming RSM in approximating the
target value because of highly non-linear experimental values. Since many responses play a key role in FDM-ed
components, recent works have focused on optimizing multiple responses simultaneously; genetic algorithms
are found to be suitable for this goal. Yang et al.50 utilized a combination of RSM and Non-dominated Sorting
Genetic Algorithm II (NSGA-II) to concurrently maximize tensile strength while minimizing surface roughness
and build time. Genetic algorithm-based optimization is also performed by Deka et al.51, using response
surface models starting from a DoE based on ANN-generated points to obtain a set of Pareto optimal solutions,
overcoming the cost and time limitations of experimental investigations.
A detailed comprehension of the influence of process parameters that rule the mechanical response of FDM-
printed parts, together with a deep understanding of the available tools for predictive and optimization goals,
is of extremely critical importance as it contributes to the industrial spread of FDM technology for structural
and functional components, especially in disciplines where the component reliability takes the lead, such as
the automotive and aerospace sectors. Indeed, industrial applications often deal with complex part geometries
and therefore adopt advanced deposition strategies to optimize the performance related with the intrinsic
FDM extrusion process. These strategies, available in the vast majority of commercial slicing softwares, create
sophisticated geometries that cannot be investigated only by considering a continuum sweep in the raster angle
of the deposited filament.
However, in FDM technical literature, no previous study has investigated the effect of categorial factors, i.e.
those characterized by discrete levels that cannot be varied in a continuum range. Among these, the deposition
strategy has probably the strongest effect, significantly affecting the mechanical properties of FDM-printed
components52,53.
Addressing this shortcoming, this work develops a Full Factorial Design with 4 factors on 3 levels: the
influence of extrusion temperature, printing speed, layer thickness, and infill pattern on the tensile behavior of
standard specimens is systematically investigated. Consequently, even though conducting a full factorial design
of experiments is costly and time-consuming54, it is the only one that provides a comprehensive understanding
of the entire interaction’s effects system considering categorial factors. A comprehensive explanation of how
above mentioned process parameters can collectively influence mechanical properties is here provided through
an ANOVA analysis derived from the FFD. The evaluated responses, derived from standard tensile tests, concern
the Ultimate Tensile Strength (UTS), elastic modulus (E), and strain at maximum stress (εd). Furthermore, the
optimal combination of inputs to maximize response variables is determined. This task is first performed through
RSM approach, usually employed for this goal, and then via Machine Learning (ML) models to overcome the
limitations of RSM sometimes falling short in capturing the intricacies of complex data. A comparative analysis
between results from RSM and from Random Forest (RF) regression model is conducted, proving the superior
performance of the latter, which is selected to serve as the fitness function for the simultaneous maximization
of the previously investigated responses integrated in NSGA-II, ultimate goal of this work. The accuracy of the
integrated ML-DoE approach is proved and validated on an extensive experimental campaign, making ML an
actual predictive tool remarkably improving the outcomes of what traditionally is performed by DoE techniques
only. Furthermore, the multi-objective optimization task allows to tune the mechanical behavior of additively
manufactured components depending on the application requirements.
The applicability of the presented methodology is of course not limited to the additive manufacturing
framework, but more generally can be extended to different research topics where the effect of many factors is
to be investigated.

Experimental plan
As already evidenced, the mechanical performance of FDM-printed components is strongly dependent on the
set of parameters adopted during the printing process. Layer thickness, extrusion temperature, printing speed,
and deposition strategy are by far the parameters with the biggest influence on the part properties; all these
factors are easily controllable by the user as their setting is required at slicing stage. Each factor is evaluated
at three different levels: as they impact the response in a non-linear way, a 34 factorial plan is chosen. Each
combination of the levels of factors define a run: four specimens are printed for each run to evaluate a mean value
in responses and, from a different perspective, the printer repeatability. Runs are 3D-printed in random order:
the full factorial plan is summarized in Table 1 together with experimental results. In particular, levels values for
layer thickness, extrusion temperature, and printing speed consist in the bottom limit, mean, and upper limit of
their ranges, which are defined by the nozzle diameter, material viscosity, and printer performance, respectively.
As for the deposition strategy, a wide variety of patterns is available in most of commercial slicing softwares.
Differently from all the available works, this study does not investigate the raster angle influence but focuses on
the infill patterns already integrated in commercial slicing softwares. These are named: i) “Lines”, corresponding
to parallel filaments with 0◦ raster angle, ii) “Grid”, with consecutive layers normal to each other at +45/ −45◦
raster angle, iii) “Cubic”, consisting in three-dimensional cubes; Figure 1 schematizes the considered deposition
patterns. As this strategies do not share a common variable that can be varied continuously between them, the
infill pattern is considered as a categorical factor, justifying the choice of a full-factorial design of experiments. A
100% infill density is kept fixed for all the specimens; external shells are avoided, as this work specifically focuses
on the effects of the infill pattern. Also, printing bed is heated up to 100◦C to help the adhesion of the first layer.
All the factors and levels are summarized in Table 2.
ANOVA

their mutual interactions, influence the resulting responses. Data from the 324 tensile tests are analyzed within
the commercial software Minitab19 to understand which of the considered factors are significant in affecting the
mechanical behavior and, if so, to evaluate the effect of the levels on the response.

Response surface method

evaluates the effects at each level of the factor. To addresses this need, the Response Surface Methodology
(RSM) is employed to explore and optimize complex relationships between input variables and output responses
by fitting experiments data with mathematical models, allowing to predict the responses also at points not
corresponding to the tested levels.

Random forest
When the factors’ effects on the considered responses are characterized by a high degree of complexity and non-
linearity, the regression via RSM can lose accuracy in predicting results. A solution to this issue can be found in
Machine Learning algorithms, which are able to progressively learn the data behavior.
Random Forest (RF)58 is an ensemble learning method consisting in an aggregation of tree predictors in
which each tree depends on the values of an independently sampled random vector of observations. At this
point it is necessary to understand if this approach is successful in improving the predictive capability for the
aim of this work; the original dataset of observations is divided into two sub-datasets, one for training and
one for testing. A cross-validation process is performed on the training dataset: it is divided into ten folds,
allowing the model to be trained on nine folds and validated on the remaining fold. This process is repeated ten
times, ensuring consistency of the methodology. After this validation, the final model is generated on the entire
training dataset and verified on the testing dataset.
In addition, Random Forests allows to understand the features’ importance by measuring how much each
feature contributes in reducing the prediction error when creating a generic decision trees in the forest. (Figure
2)
Non-dominated sorting genetic algorithm
Genetic algorithms are often used to solve complex problems in various domains, from optimizing model
parameters to searching and planning problems, exploiting the theory of natural evolution59.
In this framework, the Non-dominated Sorting Genetic Algorithm (NSGA-II) is used to find a solution
which provides the best parameters combination to simultaneously maximize the elastic modulus, the tensile
strength, and the strain at maximum stress, solving a multi-objective optimization problem. The choice for this
algorithm lies in its heuristic nature: it provides a set of optimal solutions, which correspond to the points in the
Pareto front, instead of having a single solution obtained from deterministic models.

Conclusion
This study establishes relationships between four key parameters in the 3D printing fabrication process - Infill
pattern, Extrusion temperature, Printing speed, and Layer thickness - and the most significant mechanical
properties evaluated from tensile tests: ultimate tensile strength (UTS), elastic modulus (E), and strain at
maximum stress (εd). In order to achieve this goal, a 34 full factorial design coupled with Random Forest
regressor is applied, with the latter demonstrating significantly superior predictive performance compared
to traditional Response Surface Methodology (RSM) models. The three RF models, one for each considered
mechanical response, are then incorporated into the NSGA-II algorithm, transform the analysis into a multi-
objective optimization problem.
